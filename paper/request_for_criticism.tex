\noindent
\textbf{What I am asking you to do.} Attack the construct validity of
this body of work, but constructively. I am not asking whether the
regressions are coded correctly (assume they are; the code is
reproducible) or whether the prose is polished. The defensive
question --- ``is $\dkl$ a valid innovation measure?'' --- is a dead
end, because I already suspect the honest answer is ``not in the
strong sense.'' The two questions worth your time are sharper:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Q1. If $\dkl$ is not innovation, what is it?} Give the
construct its correct name, so the downstream results mean something
precise rather than something inflated.

\textbf{Q2. How do we isolate the part of $\dkl$ that genuinely is
innovation?} Treat the observed signal as a composite and tell me
which decomposition cleanly extracts the innovation component ---
or prove no such component is identifiable from this data.
\end{quote}

My own answer to Q1, which I want you to attack: $\dkl$ measures
\textbf{lexical timing} --- whether a filing's registration language
sat \emph{ahead of} (positive) or \emph{behind} (negative) its
commercial category's vocabulary trajectory at the moment of filing.
It is not lexical novelty as such: a filing can be highly distinctive
(high on both component KLs) yet have $\dkl \approx 0$ if it is
equally far from the class's past and future. Positive $\dkl$ means
``wrote in language the field moved toward.'' That construct is
well-defined and, I think, defensible \textbf{[high]}. The open
question is \emph{why} a filing is ahead of the curve: firm
foresight (the innovation interpretation), attorney drafting fashion,
or mere exposure to a churning category. Sections 2--4 lay out the
steelman for ``it's mostly the latter two,'' my evidence, and the
decomposition program for Q2.

\section{The claims, briefly}

There are two bodies of work sharing one measurement apparatus.

\paragraph{(A) The $\dkl$ papers (\texttt{main.pdf}, \texttt{short.pdf}).}
For each of $10{,}060{,}073$ ``clean'' US trademark filings
(1990--2021 cohorts), I compute a Kullback--Leibler surprise
difference $\dkl = KL_{\text{pros}} - KL_{\text{retr}}$ on the
goods/services description text, against per-day exponentially
decay-weighted reference distributions of the same NICE class
(half-life $H = 2$y). $\dkl > 0$ when a filing's vocabulary is
unusual against the recent past and close to the near future --- the
directional resonance signal of Murdock, Allen, \& DeDeo (2017),
applied for the first time to commercially-incentivised text. The
papers claim: $\dkl$ is an innovation measure; it predicts
post-registration survival ($U$-shaped) and financial outcomes; it
is statistically independent of patent counts ($r = +0.01$ on
$174{,}569$ firm-years) and of comparable correlation magnitude;
the innovation tail aligns with the empirical content of optimal
distinctiveness and the harvest tail is an unexplained surprise.

\paragraph{(B) The dynamism / ethnic-cluster work
(\texttt{dynamism.pdf}, \texttt{ethnic\_clusters\_note.pdf}).}
Treating a firm's first-ever filing (``debut'') as a proxy for
business formation, I show trademark debuts cover $8$--$18\%$ of
Census High-Propensity Business Applications; I impute applicant
race/ethnicity from surnames; I document ethnic-cluster industries,
a Black trademark-rise / patent-fall divergence, and the collapse of
an apparent ``patent guild'' signal once foreign inventors are
stripped.

Everything in (B) inherits the validity of the measurement in (A),
plus its own surname-imputation problems. So the load-bearing
question is shared.

\section{Steelman: the case that this measures nothing real}

Here is the argument I most want you to either complete or refute. I
find it uncomfortably strong. \textbf{[moderate]} that at least the
first three links are serious and not fully answered by the current
drafts.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{A trademark is a brand, not an invention, and the
goods/services text is a legal instrument, not a description of
novelty.} The g/s description exists to define the \emph{scope of
protection}, drafted by an attorney (or selected from the USPTO's
pre-approved \emph{ID Manual}) to be as broad as the examiner will
allow. Two firms selling physically identical products can write
wildly different descriptions; a firm with a genuinely novel product
can file boilerplate. So $\dkl$ measures the novelty of the
\emph{prose}, and the prose is several steps removed from the
product. Why should I believe the prose tracks the innovation?

\item \textbf{Vocabulary novelty has at least four generators, and I
have separated none of them.} A high-$\dkl$ filing uses words that
are unusual-then-common. That could be (a) a genuine new product
category (crypto, vape, NFT); (b) legal-drafting fashion; (c)
attorneys copying each other's successful protective language; (d)
the USPTO updating its ID Manual, which mechanically shifts the
reference vocabulary for everyone. The cannabis/crypto examples I
parade are consistent with (a), but they are also exactly the cases
where new ID-Manual entries appeared. I have not shown the signal
is (a) rather than (d).

\item \textbf{The resonance interpretation was validated on a
different species of text.} Murdock, Allen, \& DeDeo (2017,
\emph{Cognition}) developed the framework on Darwin's private
reading notebooks; Barron, Huang, Spang, \& DeDeo (2018,
\emph{PNAS}) on French Revolution parliamentary debates --- in both,
the text is a window into the author's cognitive or rhetorical
state, authored by the person whose novelty is being measured. A
trademark
g/s description is drafted for protection scope by a third-party
agent. Importing the ``resonance = the field moved toward this
filing'' interpretation assumes the signal means the same thing in
a corpus where the author is a lawyer optimising a legal outcome. I
assert the analogy; I do not re-validate it.

\item \textbf{Selection: I only ever observe the $\sim 8$--$18\%$ of
business starts that trademark.} Coverage against Census
High-Propensity Business Applications is $8\%$ (2004--08) rising to
$18\%$ (2019--24). The trademark-filing population is selected on
branding intensity, consumer-facing orientation, and capitalisation.
Every sentence I write ``about firms'' is really about the
trademark-filing subset, and that subset is exactly the set of firms
for which branding-vocabulary is salient --- which is precisely the
variable $\dkl$ measures. The selection is on the regressor.

\item \textbf{``Survival'' is trademark maintenance, not firm
survival.} My headline outcome --- 5-year survival --- is the owner
paying the \S\,8 declaration-of-use fee. A live firm can abandon a
mark; a dead firm's mark can sit registered until renewal lapses.
The outcome is a portfolio-management decision correlated with, but
not identical to, firm viability. The $U$-shape on $\dkl$ could be a
$U$-shape in \emph{which marks firms bother to maintain}, not in
which firms survive.

\item \textbf{The financial validation is small and one of the
numbers is implausible.} The patent $R^2$ decomposition is on
$8{,}118$ firm-years; the gross-margin effect is $+3.2$pp per
$\sigma$ (SE $0.6$); these are real but modest. The claimed
$+28$ percentage-point 4-year excess return per $\sigma$ of debut
$\dkl$ is, on its face, \textbf{[low]} confidence and probably too
large to believe --- a $\sigma$ move in a text statistic should not
move 4-year returns by $28$pp; I suspect a specification or
sample-composition artefact and want it stress-tested.

\item \textbf{Independence from patents cuts both ways.} I sell
$r = +0.01$ with patent counts as evidence that $\dkl$ is ``a
distinct innovation channel.'' The skeptic's reading is the
opposite: $\dkl$ is uncorrelated with the one measure we
\emph{know} tracks invention because $\dkl$ is not measuring
invention at all --- it is measuring marketing/branding activity,
which is orthogonal to R\&D by construction. Independence is
necessary for ``distinct channel'' but equally consistent with
``not an innovation measure.''

\item \textbf{The ethnic / dynamism layer multiplies the problem.}
Surname imputation is probabilistic, restricted to the $\sim 32\%$
of filers parsing as individuals, static to the 2010 Census, and
weakest exactly for Black surnames (Smith/Johnson/Williams load on
both White and Black). On top of that it inherits the whole
proxy-validity question: if trademark debut is not business
formation, the coverage ratios and ethnic-composition trends
describe ``who files trademarks,'' not ``who starts businesses.''
\end{enumerate}

\section{What I actually have for validity (honest accounting)}

I am not claiming the steelman wins. Here is the evidence on the
other side, with its limits stated.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Patent independence + comparable magnitude.}
$r = +0.01$ with patent counts ($174{,}569$ firm-years); incremental
4-year-return $R^2$ of $+0.0046$ for $\dkl$ vs $+0.0024$ for
$\log(1+\text{patents})$, roughly additive. \emph{Limit:} small
$R^2$ absolute; comparable-to-patents is a low bar if patents
themselves explain little return variance in this panel.
\item \textbf{Survival is $U$-shaped under multiple
operationalisations and in $32/44$ NICE classes.} \emph{Limit:}
all of them are maintenance-based; the robustness is across
definitions of maintenance, not across independent firm-outcome
data.
\item \textbf{Cluster-viability mediation absorbs $84\%$ of the
quadratic.} This is the one result that looks mechanism-like rather
than mechanical. \emph{Limit:} the mediator is itself built from
trademark data (cosine-neighbour survival), so it does not break out
of the corpus.
\item \textbf{Coverage brackets Dinlersoz et al.\ (2018).} Our
$8$--$18\%$ spans their restricted-LBD $10$--$15\%$. \emph{Limit:}
agreement on the \emph{coverage fraction} is not validation that the
covered filings \emph{mean} anything; it just says we count debut
filers the way Census counts them.
\item \textbf{Face validity.} Canonical winners skew positive
$\dkl$; flux-neutral tokens (\texttt{organisational},
\texttt{conformity}) die. \emph{Limit:} selected ex-post; not a
test.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
My own read \textbf{[moderate]}: I can defend ``$\dkl$ is a
nontrivial, patent-orthogonal signal that predicts trademark-level
outcomes and correlates weakly with firm financials.'' I cannot
currently defend ``$\dkl$ is an innovation measure'' against a
determined skeptic, because every outcome I validate against is
either inside the trademark corpus or a small noisy financial panel.

\section{Q1: if not innovation, what is it? --- four candidate names}

A ladder of constructs from strong to weak. Tell me which is the
correct name for what $\dkl$ measures.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*]
\item[(I) Strong --- ``$\dkl$ is an innovation measure.''] The
current framing. Requires that vocabulary novelty in g/s prose
tracks product/market innovation. My confidence this survives
review as stated: \textbf{[low]}.
\item[(II) Medium --- ``$\dkl$ is a leading indicator of commercial
category emergence.''] Claims the corpus-level signal (which
\emph{vocabularies} are forming and being adopted) is real and
useful, while staying agnostic about whether any individual filer is
``innovative.'' The cannabis/crypto/NFT waves are the evidence; the
firm-level survival/returns become secondary. Confidence:
\textbf{[moderate]}.
\item[(III) Weak/descriptive --- ``$\dkl$ measures the linguistic
position of a filing relative to its NICE class's vocabulary
trajectory.''] Purely descriptive. Every downstream correlation
(survival, returns, ethnic clusters) becomes an empirical regularity
to be explained, not a validation of an innovation construct. This
is almost certainly defensible \textbf{[high]} but gives up the
headline.
\item[(IV) Activity proxy --- ``a trademark debut is formal
commercial activity with branding intent.''] For the dynamism work
specifically: not innovation, not business formation \emph{per se},
but a measurable, name-disclosed, near-real-time signal of
branding-intent commercial activity, explicitly the $8$--$18\%$
branding-intensive slice of formation. Confidence: \textbf{[moderate]}.
\end{description}

\noindent
My current bet \textbf{[moderate]}: the correct primitive name is
(III) lexical timing; (II) is the defensible \emph{corpus-level}
claim built on top of it; (I) is only licensed for the subset of
$\dkl$ that survives the isolation program in the next section; and
(IV) is what the dynamism work should stand on, needing none of the
above. The restructure I am tempted by: lead with (III) as the
measurement contribution, establish (II) with the category-emergence
evidence, present firm-level survival/financial results as
\emph{regularities under (III) awaiting the (I) decomposition}, and
let the dynamism work rest on (IV). Tell me if that is rigor or
cowardice.

\section{Q2: how do we isolate the innovation component?}

Model the observed statistic as a composite:
\[
\dkl_{\text{filing}} = \underbrace{\beta\,\text{(firm innovation)}}_{\text{the part we want}}
  + \underbrace{\gamma\,\text{(attorney/drafting style)}}_{\text{fashion}}
  + \underbrace{\delta\,\text{(category churn exposure)}}_{\text{being in a hot class}}
  + \varepsilon.
\]
The whole question is whether $\beta\,\text{(firm innovation)}$ is
separately identifiable. Five tests, ranked by decisiveness $\times$
feasibility. Tell me which is decisive, which is hopeless, and what
I am missing.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*]
\item[(C) Within-firm $\dkl$ vs.\ patent timing --- \emph{runnable
now}.] For firms that both patent and trademark, test whether a
firm's high-$\dkl$ trademark years coincide with its high-patent
years. Within-firm temporal co-movement is hard to explain by
drafting fashion or category churn and would be direct evidence
$\beta > 0$. Temporal independence would say $\dkl$ and invention
are unrelated \emph{even inside the same firm}. We have
\texttt{firm\_dkl\_vs\_patents.parquet}; I can run this immediately.
\item[(D) Term-provenance decomposition --- \emph{runnable now}.]
Split each filing's $\dkl$ contribution into tokens
\emph{new to the class} vs.\ recombined pre-existing tokens. Genuine
novelty should load on new tokens; drafting fashion on unusual
recombination of old tokens. If the survival/return effects
concentrate in the new-token component, that is the innovation part
isolated. We have the per-class vocab and per-filing tokens.
\item[(A) Attorney / correspondent fixed effects --- \emph{cheap
re-parse}.] The strongest design. The USPTO XML carries the
correspondent/attorney of record; we already re-parsed once for
applicant country, so adding the attorney field is incremental.
Within-attorney variation in $\dkl$ nets out $\gamma$ (drafting
style) by construction. If the survival/financial effects survive
attorney fixed effects, $\dkl$ is not drafting fashion.
\item[(B) ID-Manual-only vs.\ free-text split --- \emph{moderate}.]
The USPTO \emph{ID Manual} is a controlled vocabulary of
pre-approved descriptions. Filings using only ID-Manual language
have mechanically constrained vocabulary ($\dkl$ is pure $\delta$,
category-churn exposure); filings with free-text descriptions carry
discretionary language that can encode $\beta$. If the effects
concentrate in free-text filings, that localises the innovation
component. Needs the ID Manual (downloadable).
\item[(E) ID-Manual adoption prediction --- \emph{moderate}.] The
institutional validation test: does a high-$\dkl$ filing use terms
that the ID Manual \emph{later} codifies? If filings that are
``ahead of the curve'' by $\dkl$ are using terms the USPTO itself
subsequently standardises, the leadership is real and externally
validated, not an artefact of my reference window. Needs ID-Manual
version history.
\end{description}

\noindent
My plan, pending your verdict: run (C) and (D) now, add the attorney
field on the next re-parse for (A), and treat (B)/(E) as the
ID-Manual work package. If (C) shows within-firm co-movement and the
(D) new-token component carries the survival effect, I will claim
(I) \emph{for that isolated component only} and keep (III) as the
name for the rest. If both come back null, (I) is dead and I retreat
to (III)+(II)+(IV).

\section{Specific questions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item Is (A) attorney fixed effects actually clean, or does the
firm choose its attorney partly \emph{for} drafting style, so that
attorney FE absorbs real firm-level signal along with the fashion?
\item Is (C) within-firm $\dkl$/patent co-movement convincing
evidence of $\beta > 0$, or is there a confound (e.g., both spike
when the firm enters a new product line) that co-movement cannot
rule out?
\item For (D): is ``new-to-class token'' the right operationalisation
of the innovation component, or should novelty be measured against
the whole-corpus vocabulary rather than the class?
\item Is there an external, non-trademark, non-financial outcome to
validate survival against --- business-registration death,
UCC-lien termination, Form-D withdrawal, domain expiry --- that
breaks the maintenance-vs-survival circularity?
\item Does the $+28$pp/$\sigma$ return number kill my credibility on
sight? Drop it pending re-estimation?
\item For the ethnic work: is surname imputation usable given the
Black-surname cross-loading (Smith/Johnson/Williams), or do I
restrict to the discriminating Hispanic and API analyses and demote
Black claims to a flagged-caveat appendix?
\end{enumerate}

\section{First isolation results: lead-lag, cross-class transfer, case studies}

Three of the Q2 tests have first results. They land on a sharper
picture than either the strong (I) or full-retreat (III) framing
anticipated. The headline: $\dkl$ tracks invention activity at the
\emph{between-firm} level but not (cleanly) within-firm at annual
resolution, and an idea-transfer signature is visible in the
filing-level vocabulary data.

\subsection*{Lead-lag: patents \emph{do} lead high-$\dkl$ trademarks
between firms, but not within firms at this resolution}

The pipeline framing (patents $\to$ products $\to$ trademarks)
predicts that contemporaneous patent--$\dkl$ correlation can be
zero while lagged correlation is positive. We rebuilt an outer-join
firm $\times$ year panel restricted to patent-active firms ($n =
54{,}323$ firms with $\geq 1$ patent in any year), keyed patents to
\emph{application} date (the invention-timing proxy) rather than
grant date, and computed
$\mathrm{corr}(\text{patents}(t),\, Y(t+k))$ for $k = -5\ldots+5$,
where $Y$ is either the firm's mean $\dkl$ or its count of
positive-$\dkl$ filings that year.

\paragraph{The compositional caveat.} Within-firm FE regressions of
the same outcomes on patent-application lags $0$--$3$, clustered SE,
restricted to firms with $\geq 5$ firm-years of trademark $\dkl$
($n = 1{,}146$ firms, $13{,}425$ firm-years), produce
\emph{statistically insignificant} coefficients across all lags
(largest $|t| \approx 1.7$, all $p > 0.08$).  Direction on
positive-$\dkl$ count is consistent with the pipeline (lag-$1$
coef $+0.014$, lag-$2$ coef $+0.014$) but neither passes
significance.

What this means \textbf{[high]}: the population-level lead-lag is
\emph{between-firm}. Patent-active firms file systematically more
positive-$\dkl$ trademarks than non-patent firms, and the
between-firm covariance accumulates across years to produce the
$r = +0.18$ at $k = +1$. \emph{Within} a firm, knowing how many
patents it filed last year does not predict how many positive-$\dkl$
trademarks it will file this year, at annual resolution and on this
sample.

\paragraph{Interpretation for Q1 and Q2.} The straight pipeline
narrative (``last year's patent caused this year's trademark'') is
not supported as a within-firm temporal mechanism in our data. What
\emph{is} supported is firm-level co-selection: the population of
patent-active firms overlaps with the population of high-$\dkl$
trademark filers, and the lead-lag profile of the population
covariance is consistent with the broad direction of the pipeline
(patents earlier, trademarks later). The conservative
reformulation: $\dkl$-positive trademarks identify the same set of
firms that patenting identifies, with a roughly $+1$-year lag at the
population level, but $\dkl$ does \emph{not} cleanly time-resolve a
within-firm innovation pulse. This is closer to reframing~(I) being
partially licensed --- for the patent-overlap subset, $\dkl$ does
co-localise with invention activity --- than to either~(III) being
the full retreat or~(I) being licensed in the strong causal sense.

\paragraph{Reasons the within-firm test may underpower a real
pipeline.} $n = 1{,}146$ firms is small; annual resolution may be
too coarse (patent-to-launch cycles are often months, not years);
patents come in bunches; not every patent leads to a trademark; and
the relationship is plausibly heterogeneous across industries
(pharma vs.\ software vs.\ hardware) which we have not stratified.
Higher-frequency timing data (monthly patent applications $\to$
monthly trademark filings) and within-CPC-to-NICE stratification
would be the next steps.

\subsection*{Cross-class vocabulary transfer: positive $\dkl$ comes at the cost of registration}

A separate test of what $\dkl$ measures: identify trademark filings
whose goods/services prose imports tokens recently ascendant in a
\emph{different} NICE class (vocabulary moving across industry
boundaries). On $7{,}290{,}115$ clean-panel filings, with the
operational definition ``a filing imports $\geq 2$ tokens currently
rising in some other class but not yet rising in its own,'' transfer
filings have $\mathbf{higher\ \dkl}$ ($+0.033$ pooled, $+0.031$ within
class$\times$year cell) and \emph{simultaneously}
$\mathbf{lower\ registration}$ rate ($-4.4$pp pooled, $-2.8$pp within
cell, both $z > 11$). The face-validity examples are correct: the
top-intensity transfer filings are $2014$--$2015$ vape and
e-cigarette filings importing class-$34$ (tobacco) vocabulary into
classes $1, 10, 11, 35, 38$.

This is the cleanest result on Q1 so far. Cross-class imports both
\emph{look novel} (positive $\dkl$, because they carry tokens that
are unusual against the destination class's recent past) and
\emph{fare worse at the examination gate} (lower registration,
because examiners and prior-art collisions disfavour them). Both
priors I considered turn out to be partially right \emph{at the same
time}: validated-novelty and category-mismatch are simultaneous
forces, not alternatives. Detail: see \texttt{paper/cross\_class\_transfer\_note.pdf}.

\subsection*{Firm case studies: the pipeline is visible firm-by-firm even when the panel won't show it}

A complementary qualitative check on eight IP-heavy firms (Apple,
Tesla, NVIDIA, Pfizer, Moderna, Dyson, Amazon, Salesforce). The
cleanest patent$\to$trademark linkages, where the within-firm
pipeline is visible by inspection:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
\item \textbf{NVIDIA TEGRA ($2008$, $\dkl = +0.42$), DGX ($2017$,
$+0.46$), RTX ($2018$, $+0.28$)} --- three textbook technology
discontinuities (mobile SoCs, AI compute, ray tracing), each landing
a $> +0.25$ $\dkl$ on the firm's own trademark filing at the time
of the corresponding patent-cluster activity.
\item \textbf{Tesla 2015 firm-mean $\dkl = +0.33$, $n=12$ filings},
the firm's largest single-year $\dkl$ spike, coincident with the
Powerwall and Powerpack stationary-storage launches.
\item \textbf{Amazon KINDLE ($2006$, $+0.19$), ECHO ($2014$,
$+0.16$), PRIME AIR ($2014$, $+0.15$)} --- three sequential
novel-vocabulary filings tracking Amazon's hardware push.
\end{itemize}
Honest negative findings: Pfizer's pipeline is broken by IP-holdco
filings (Warner-Lambert / Wyeth / BioNTech as legal owners); Apple
\texttt{IPHONE} ($1996$) is a parked filing $11$ years pre-launch,
unrelated to the eventual product; Salesforce \texttt{SLACK} is
acquisition-conditional. Detail: \texttt{paper/firm\_case\_studies\_note.pdf}.

\paragraph{What the case studies and the panel jointly say.} For the
right kind of firm (hardware-with-a-clear-new-product-name and not a
holdco) the within-firm pipeline is visible by eye --- NVIDIA is
near-perfect. The panel regression's within-firm null is therefore
\emph{not} ``no firm exhibits the pipeline,'' it is ``averaging
across $1{,}146$ firms with $\geq 5$ TM-$\dkl$-years does not
recover an annual-resolution lag coefficient.'' Both are honest. The
question for you (reviewer): is the right test stratified by firm
type (hardware vs.\ pharma vs.\ software) and at higher temporal
resolution, or is the population-level lead-lag $+ $ case-study
qualitative evidence enough?

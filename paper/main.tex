\begin{abstract}
We propose a Kullback--Leibler surprise difference $\Delta KL =
KL_{\text{pros}} - KL_{\text{retr}}$ as an innovation measure for
trademark filings: $\Delta KL > 0$ when a filing's goods/services
vocabulary is prospectively novel (unusual against the recent past)
and retrospectively obvious (similar to the near future) --- the
directional resonance signal of Murdock, Allen \& DeDeo
(\emph{Cognition}, 2017) applied to commercially incentivised text.
We compute $\Delta KL$ on each of the $16.5$ million U.S.\ trademark
filings since 1985 against per-day exponentially decay-weighted
reference distributions of the same NICE class (half-life $H = 2$
years; chosen by signal-maximising sweep). The hypothesis going in
was that firms with positive $\Delta KL$ would outperform on
standard measures of firm success, and that $\Delta KL$ would be
additive to the standard patent-count innovation proxy. Both
predictions are confirmed: $\Delta KL$ predicts 4-year stock
excess returns and gross margin conditional on positive $\Delta KL$,
is essentially uncorrelated with patent counts ($r = +0.01$ on
$174{,}569$ firm-years), and is sub-additive with patents in the
$R^2$ decomposition --- it indexes a distinct innovation channel.

\textbf{Two response curves, two theories.} The innovation side of
$\Delta KL$ (positive) is itself a clean saturating-exponential
response: $P(\text{survived}\,5y) = a + b\,e^{-c\,\Delta KL}$ with
$a = 0.995$, $b = -0.55$, $c = 0.063$ on the $5$M-debut panel ($R^2 =
0.499$ across $20$ bins), concave-down, no inverted-$U$ inflection.
This is the empirical content of optimal distinctiveness
(Deephouse 1999, \emph{SMJ}; Zhao et al.\ 2017, \emph{SMJ}) translated
from \emph{level} of category-typicality to \emph{directional}
vocabulary flux: novelty pays with diminishing returns. The
\textbf{new finding} is that filings at the \emph{opposite} flux
extreme --- prospectively obvious and retrospectively novel,
$\Delta KL < 0$ --- have their own saturating-exponential response,
fitted much more tightly ($a = 0.573$, $b = -0.137$, $c = 3.33$,
$R^2 = 0.978$). On the harvest side, survival \emph{increases} with
$|\Delta KL|$ at a much faster rate and saturates closer to zero:
the deeper a debut filing sits into the receding legacy vocabulary
of its category, the more durable its mark. We read this as
\emph{increasing returns to late-stage imitation}, complementary to
the diminishing-returns innovation curve. Together the two
saturating exponentials produce the survival $U$ on $\Delta KL$ in
the pooled corpus. The pattern holds at the per-industry level in
$32$ of $44$ NICE classes; only $1$ industry shows a significant
inverted-$U$; $11$ are indeterminate. Token-level survival rates
corroborate the U at the corpus level: negative-stratum tokens
(\texttt{microfiche}, \texttt{dial-up}, \texttt{pager},
\texttt{lager}, \texttt{annuity}) and positive-stratum tokens
(\texttt{blockchain}, \texttt{cannabidiol}, \texttt{non-fungible})
both survive well above the corpus baseline; flux-neutral tokens
(\texttt{organisational}, \texttt{conformity}, \texttt{freighting})
survive at $11$--$26\%$ vs.\ a corpus baseline of $\sim 49\%$. In
our hand-curated panel of $41$ canonical ``winner'' firms,
$37/41$ have positive firm-mean $\Delta KL$. What the mechanism on
the harvest side is --- intelligent harvesting of vanishing
concepts, recognition of emerging ones, or selection on an
unobservable correlate --- is not separable in the trademark record;
what is unambiguous is that filings whose vocabulary places them
squarely \emph{on trend} in the centre of the distribution survive at
the lowest rate in the corpus.

\textbf{Novelty of language and novelty of claim are different signals.}
A pioneer-ladder analysis on five category-defining buzzwords
(\texttt{website}, \texttt{wireless}, \texttt{online retail},
\texttt{social media}, \texttt{blockchain}) ranks every firm in the
corpus by date-of-first-adoption; the very-first $10$--$50$ adopters
do badly, the fast-follower band at ranks $50$--$1{,}000$ has
$1.5$--$4\times$ the late-entrant IPO rate, and the mass late wave
does worst. Canonical winners are mostly absent from the very-early
ranks (Amazon $174$\textsuperscript{th} on \texttt{online retail},
Coinbase $96$\textsuperscript{th} on \texttt{blockchain}; Uber, Lyft,
Airbnb never used their category buzzword at all) but most have
positive first-filing $\Delta KL$, often strongly so (Oracle $+4.39$
nats, Intel $+3.26$, Microsoft $+2.99$, eBay $+1.73$, Apple $+1.68$,
Coinbase $+0.98$, Lyft $+0.76$, Twitter $+0.75$, Uber $+0.70$,
LinkedIn $+0.58$, Salesforce $+0.43$). The two ``novelties'' decouple:
the novelty of the \emph{claim} (which vocabulary the firm adopts) and
the novelty of the \emph{language} (the texture of their
goods/services prose) are independent levers.

$\Delta KL$ is essentially uncorrelated with the dominant alternative
innovation proxy (firm-year patent counts, $r = +0.010$ on $174{,}569$
firm-years), positively correlated with post-registration survival,
and positively correlated with profitability among publicly-traded
firms ($+3.2$ pp gross margin per $\sigma$, SE $0.6$ pp).
\end{abstract}

\section{Background}

Innovation is a central object of empirical economic and management
research, but the construct is hard to measure. The dominant quantitative
approaches are R\&D expenditure (an input proxy that rewards spend rather
than novelty), patent counts and patent citations (which require
strategic legal filing, and which Trajtenberg, Hagedoorn, and others have
shown to be at best a noisy proxy), and the market value premium of equity
over book value (which conflates innovation with every other investor
perception). Survey-based scales such as the Community Innovation Survey
and Gatignon et al.'s instrument capture richer constructs but suffer from
common-method bias and limited sample size. The lack of agreement on what
to measure is itself a finding: across measures, pairwise correlations are
typically $\rho \approx 0.3$.

We propose using trademark filings as a complementary novelty channel.
Trademarks have several useful properties that patents do not: applicants
file a single, brief, machine-readable goods-and-services description that
is sharply incentivized to be exact (broad enough to protect future use,
narrow enough to clear examination); the data are released into the
public domain by the USPTO without lag; and the universe of filers is
broader than the universe of patentees, including most consumer-facing
firms. The cost is that trademarks measure novelty in the
goods-and-services lexicon, not in the underlying technology --- a brand
that solves an old problem with a new identity (e.g.\ \textsc{Liquid Death}'s
canned water) is largely invisible.

Our broader motivation is to ask whether innovative firms are rewarded or
punished. Two pieces of folk wisdom are in tension. The first, conveyed
in the phrase ``you can tell the pioneers by the arrows in their backs,''
is that firms that depart from convention bear higher search and adoption
costs, get out-competed by fast-followers, and underperform on average.
The second, expressed in the older saying ``build a better mousetrap and
the world will beat a path to your door,'' is that meaningful novelty
attracts customers and rewards its inventor with durable advantage. Both
can be true simultaneously: novelty might raise variance --- some
pioneers fail spectacularly, others enjoy durable category leadership.
This paper offers an empirical test of that joint pattern using the full
USPTO trademark backfile.

\section{Data}

The trademark corpus is the full backfile published by the USPTO Open
Data Portal, current through end-of-year 2025. After filtering to
filings with non-empty goods/services text in NICE classes 1--45 and
a parseable filing date, the corpus contains
\textbf{$16{,}526{,}850$ filings} from \textbf{$5{,}673{,}680$ unique
registrant names} across all 45 NICE international classes (35 goods
classes plus 10 services classes), spanning 1985 to early 2026. Each
record carries the registrant's name as it appeared on the
application (this is the owner at the time of filing; subsequent
assignments are not reflected), the filing date, a status code that
records the current legal state of the mark, an optional registration
date, the NICE class assignments, and one or more goods-and-services
descriptive statements. Statements describing the visual mark itself
rather than the business (USPTO type-code prefix D) are excluded;
only goods-and-services statements (prefix GS) are kept. Two classes
are presented in narrative detail throughout the paper: NICE Class~9
(Software \& Electronics) and NICE Class~32 (Beer \& Soft Drinks).
The remaining 43 industries are summarized in
Section~\ref{sec:cross}, and per-class summary statistics for the two
focal classes appear in Appendix~\ref{app:summary}.

The financial-outcome data are the SEC EDGAR Financial Statement
Data Sets, 64 quarterly XBRL releases spanning fiscal years 2009
through 2024, covering $13{,}127$ unique CIKs that filed at least
one 10-K. We extract revenue, cost of revenue, gross profit, R\&D
expense, operating income, and net income.

\paragraph{Inclusion filter (\textsc{clean}).}
Almost every analysis in this paper is restricted to the same
inclusion filter, which we call \textsc{clean}. The filter has five
clauses, each addressing a separate failure mode: two outcome-window
clauses (\texttt{year} bounds) that ensure the survival outcome is
fully observed and that the prospective reference window is non-empty,
two density floors on the per-class reference distributions
(\texttt{n\_ref\_pros} and \texttt{n\_ref\_retr} $\geq 1{,}000$) that
ensure $KL$ scores reflect a real vocabulary distribution rather than
small-sample noise, and a per-filing token-count floor
($n_{\text{terms}} \geq 3$) that prevents 1- or 2-token descriptions
from producing degenerate $KL$ values. Of $16{,}530{,}448$ raw
filings with parsed surprise data, $\textbf{10{,}060{,}073}$
($60.9\%$) survive the full filter and form the analytic corpus.

The dominant binder is the year window, $[1990, 2021]$. The lower
bound is the literal arithmetic burn-in described in the Method
section (the corpus begins in 1984 and we leave a margin for the
decay reference). The upper bound is the cohort cap: with a 2026
freeze date and a 5-year post-registration survival window, filings
must be from 2021 or earlier for \texttt{survived\_5y} to be
observable. The token-count floor, $n_{\text{terms}} \geq 3$, is the
only other clause that uniquely excludes a meaningful share ($+745$k
filings rescued if dropped); when we look at the templated
short-description analysis in Section~\ref{sec:templated-survival} we
report results both with and without this clause, since the most
heavily templated G/S strings (\textit{e.g.}~``restaurant services'',
``legal services'') are 2-token strings and would otherwise be
excluded.

\paragraph{Registration is determined by status code, not registration
date.} We classify a mark as having ``completed registration'' from its
USPTO status code (6xx live-registered or 8xx post-registration-cancelled),
not from the \texttt{registration\_date} field, which is unreliable in
older records (mostly pre-2003): many records with a valid registered
status code carry a missing \texttt{registration\_date} in the
published XML. The pipeline that extracts and analyses these data is
published with the paper; technical details live in the companion
repository.

\section{Method}

For each filing $i$ at year $t$ with goods-and-services text $w_i$ we
construct a term-frequency distribution $P_i$ over a fixed dictionary $V$
of unigram and bigram tokens. The dictionary is built once per NICE class
with a custom analyzer that splits each description at semicolons before
tokenizing, so bigrams cannot cross goods/services-item boundaries. We
retain only unigrams and bigrams that appear in at least $50$ distinct
filings (documents) and in no more than $50\%$ of all filings, on the
view that very rare tokens are typos and very common tokens are USPTO
boilerplate.

For each filing $i$ at filing-date $t_i$ we form two reference
distributions by per-day exponential decay-weighting of every other
filing in the same NICE class:
\begin{align}
Q_i^{\text{pros}}(v) &\propto \alpha + \sum_{j:\, t_j < t_i}
  e^{-\lambda_{\text{pros}}\,(t_i - t_j)}\,\mathrm{count}(v, j), \\
Q_i^{\text{retr}}(v) &\propto \alpha + \sum_{j:\, t_j > t_i}
  e^{-\lambda_{\text{retr}}\,(t_j - t_i)}\,\mathrm{count}(v, j),
\end{align}
with $\lambda = \ln(2)/H$ and \textbf{half-life $H = 2$ years} on both
sides and Laplace smoothing $\alpha = 1.0$. The half-life is chosen by
a sweep over $H \in \{0.5, 1, 2, 3, 5\}$ years on Class~9 that
maximises corr$(\Delta KL, \text{survived\_5y})$ and the logit
pseudo-$R^2$ on the same outcome (Figure~\ref{fig:halflife-signal}).
Density on the reference distributions is reported as Kish effective
sample size $N_{\text{eff}} = (\sum_j w_j)^2 / \sum_j w_j^2$; we
restrict all filing-level analyses to filings with
$N_{\text{eff}} \geq 1{,}000$ on both sides.

The prospective and retrospective surprise of filing $i$ are the
support-restricted KL divergences
\[
KL_i^{\text{pros}} = \sum_{v: P_i(v)>0} P_i(v) \, \log\frac{P_i(v)}{Q_i^{\text{pros}}(v)},
\quad
KL_i^{\text{retr}} = \sum_{v: P_i(v)>0} P_i(v) \, \log\frac{P_i(v)}{Q_i^{\text{retr}}(v)}.
\]
With $\log$ in natural units, $KL_i$ is in nats. A useful intuition:
$KL_i^{\text{pros}}$ is the expected log-odds per token that a reader
who had seen only the filing's recent past would assign to the filing's
own term distribution versus the past's. $KL_i = 3$ nats means a typical
token in this filing is $e^3 \approx 20\times$ more probable under the
filing's own distribution than under the recent corpus. KL is sensitive
to filing length (short filings with one rare token can score high
mechanically); every regression in the paper controls for
$\log(n_\text{terms})$ and most report $z$-scored $\Delta KL$.

The directional flux score is $\Delta KL_i = KL_i^{\text{pros}} -
KL_i^{\text{retr}}$: the change between how unlike the recent past a
filing reads and how unlike the near future it reads. Positive
$\Delta KL$ means the filing's vocabulary is closer to the future than
to the past (\emph{flux-positive}; the field migrated toward this
filing); negative $\Delta KL$ means closer to the past than to the
future (\emph{flux-negative}; the field migrated away).

\paragraph{Magnitudes.}
To ground the reader: across the all-class analytic pool of $13.0$M
filings with $N_{\text{eff}} \geq 1{,}000$ on both sides, the 5th,
50th, and 95th percentiles are
$KL^{\text{pros}} = [2.99,\, 4.85,\, 7.20]$ nats (std $1.29$) and
$KL^{\text{retr}} = [2.98,\, 4.84,\, 7.22]$ nats (std $1.30$). The two
components are correlated at $\rho = +0.99$ pooled across all classes
(see Table~\ref{tab:components}); they measure the same
general-distinctiveness signal in two temporal directions, and their
per-filing levels track each other tightly. Because they share that
much distinctiveness against the per-class boilerplate, their
difference $\Delta KL$ has a markedly tighter range: 5th/50th/95th
percentiles $[-0.30,\, -0.00,\, +0.34]$ nats (std $0.22$, median
essentially zero by construction of the look-behind / look-ahead
window). A filing in the top 5\% of $\Delta KL$ is therefore $\sim
0.6$ nats more flux-positive than one in the bottom 5\%, on a scale
where each component routinely exceeds $5$ nats outright. The
substantive direction lives in this comparatively small flux
residual, not in the much larger common distinctiveness factor.

\paragraph{Identical-text filings are signal.} Trademark practice rewards
templated language. Many breweries file the boilerplate token
\texttt{Beer}; many app developers file \texttt{downloadable software}.
We do not deduplicate identical text: filings that use the same words
\emph{should} receive the same score, because deviations from the
boilerplate are themselves the signal.

\paragraph{Burn-in window.}
The decay reference distributions need filings on both sides of $t_i$
for the weighted sums to be well populated. The $N_{\text{eff}} \geq
1{,}000$ density filter above automatically excludes small NICE
classes' early-decade cohorts and the corpus right-edge filings whose
forward window is too sparse. For regressions on the panel we restrict
to filings from \textbf{1990 onward} (after the corpus's left-edge
digitisation boundary in 1984 plus a margin to populate the
look-behind) and to filings whose year has at least 5 years of forward
observation, currently \textbf{1990--2021} given a 2026 freeze date.
Figure~\ref{fig:burnin} shades the two burn-in windows.

\subsection*{What $\Delta KL$ measures (and what it does not)}
\label{sec:flux-not-distinctiveness}

The two component KLs are very highly correlated: per-filing
$\rho(KL^{\text{pros}}, KL^{\text{retr}}) = 0.99$ on the pooled clean
panel. A reader plotting any outcome against either component
univariately sees nearly identical curves because the two component
levels are, to first order, the same number --- both measuring
\emph{distinctiveness} (how unusual the filing's vocabulary is against
the rest of the corpus, in either temporal direction).

The PCA decomposition of $(KL^{\text{pros}}, KL^{\text{retr}})$ makes
the geometry explicit:
\begin{align*}
\text{PC1} \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(KL^{\text{pros}} + KL^{\text{retr}})
  &\;\;\;\text{= general distinctiveness} \\
\text{PC2} \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(KL^{\text{pros}} - KL^{\text{retr}})
  &\;\;\;\propto \Delta KL = \text{vocabulary flux}
\end{align*}

$\Delta KL$ is the orthogonal residual after PC1 is removed; it is the
\emph{only} part of the joint signal with a forward/backward direction.
PC1 says ``this filing is unusual.'' PC2 says ``this filing is unusual
\emph{and the field's vocabulary subsequently moved toward it rather
than away}.'' We claim throughout that the substantive innovation
signal lives in PC2; PC1 is largely a length/rarity covariate. The
closest-adjacent management-theory framework, optimal distinctiveness
(Deephouse 1999, \emph{SMJ}; Zhao et al.\ 2017, \emph{SMJ}), predicts
an inverted-U on the \emph{level} of distinctiveness. The framework we
are extending is DeDeo's prospective/retrospective surprise scheme
(Murdock, Allen \& DeDeo 2017, \emph{Cognition}; Barron, Huang, Spang
\& DeDeo 2018, \emph{PNAS}), which decomposes a temporal corpus into
novelty, transience, and resonance and which we apply for the first
time to commercially incentivised text.

\section{What the components do separately}
\label{sec:components}

Before introducing $\Delta KL$ as a single scalar, it is worth asking
whether the prospective and retrospective KL components carry
independent information about a filing. A natural prior --- and one we
held coming in --- is that they should pull in opposite directions:
prospective surprise should make a filing harder to clear examination
(novel goods/services language draws office actions), while
retrospective surprise should reward the filer for ``being right'' (the
future ends up resembling them, suggesting they understood a market
others did not). Under that view, the value of $\Delta KL$ is that it
nets two opposing forces.

\textbf{The data do not support that decomposition.} Table~\ref{tab:components}
shows that the per-filing correlation between $KL_{\text{pros}}$ and
$KL_{\text{retr}}$ is $+0.97$ in software (Class 9), $+0.93$ in beverages
(Class 32), and $+0.97$ in the 11.4-million-filing all-class pool. The
two scores are computed against the same per-filing distribution
$P_i(v)$, with the only difference being whether the reference window
is the five preceding or the five following years; the bulk of variance
in either KL therefore comes from $P_i$ itself, and only a thin sliver
comes from the past-vs-future shift in the reference. As a consequence,
plotted against any per-filing outcome the two components produce
nearly identical curves.

The curves themselves are not lines and not exponentials. They are
\textbf{monotone-saturating}: registration probability rises from about
$0.43$ at $KL = 3$ nats to about $0.53$ at $KL = 7.5$ nats in software,
and similarly $0.46 \to 0.59$ in beverages, with most of the rise
concentrated in the moderate range and the curve flattening at the
extremes (Figure~\ref{fig:components-perfiling-sw}; beverage and pooled
versions live in
\texttt{paper/results/per\_filing\_pros\_vs\_retr\_\{beverages,all\}.png}).
At the firm-mean level the same outcomes against the same components
trace inverted-U arcs in software (peak survival and peak public-listing
both at firm-mean $KL \approx 5$ nats; tails decline in both directions);
beverages produces a hump on survival and a monotone decline on
public-listing (high-novelty beverage firms are systematically less
likely to be public --- they are craft brewers, not Coca-Cola). The
firm-level views are in
\texttt{paper/results/pros\_vs\_retr\_curves\_\{software,beverages,all\}.png}.

\paragraph{The sharp implication.} Because the components are nearly
collinear, the signal in their \emph{difference}
$\Delta KL_i = KL_i^{\text{pros}} - KL_i^{\text{retr}}$ is a small
share of either component's variance --- but it is the only part of the
joint $(KL_{\text{pros}}, KL_{\text{retr}})$ score that does not cancel
out under the shared-$P_i$ structure. A filing whose
language was more unusual against the past than against the near future
is one whose language the near future ended up resembling. The shared
``how unusual was the language at all'' term cancels; what remains is
``did the unusualness fade because others adopted it.'' The rest of the
paper takes that residual --- the imitated-innovator score --- as the
primary axis. Software and beverages stay as our running examples
through Outcomes 1--3; the back matter establishes that the basic story
holds across the other 43 NICE classes.

\section{Face validity}
\label{sec:fv}

Figure~\ref{fig:quadrant} plots each filing in the prospective--retrospective
KL plane. The horizontal axis (prospective KL) measures dissimilarity from
filings in the previous five years (high $\Rightarrow$ less like the past);
the vertical axis (retrospective KL) measures dissimilarity from filings in
the next five years (high $\Rightarrow$ less like the future). Filings
\emph{below} the diagonal $KL_{\text{pros}} = KL_{\text{retr}}$ are
innovators by our criterion --- the past does a worse job of predicting
them than the future does. Filings above the diagonal are tired. The
labelled marks include expectation-confirming category-creators
(\textsc{OpenAI} 2016, \textsc{Claude} 2023, \textsc{Instagram} 2011,

\section{Canonical winners and failed firms as KL profiles}
\label{sec:canonical-winners}

A direct face-validity check: take a curated list of widely-recognised
firms from the last fifty years, find their first post-founding
trademark filing, compute the firm-mean $\Delta KL$ across all their
filings, and compare against a control list of widely-recognised
\emph{failed} firms. If the construct is meaningful, the winners should
read as forward-flux (positive $\Delta KL$) and the failures should
spread out --- some failed despite being trend-setters, some failed
\emph{because} they were exploiting a receding vocabulary.

\paragraph{Caveat on entity matching.}
The trademark XML records each filing's owner only as a free-text
\texttt{owner\_name} string with no entity identifier, so any
``firm $\to$ all its filings'' join is necessarily heuristic. For the
table below we use hand-curated regular expressions that require the
brand string to co-occur with a corporate suffix (\texttt{Inc.},
\texttt{LLC}, \texttt{Corporation}, \texttt{Technologies}, etc.) and we
restrict each firm's panel to filings on or after its founding year.
The regexes are not perfect: we are confident that at least some hits
are entity collisions and we have tightened individual regexes where we
identified collisions. Amazon's regex, for example, matches only the
\texttt{Amazon.com} variants because the string ``Amazon Technologies,
Inc.'' was used as a registered owner by a different firm in
1994--1995, before Amazon's IP-holding subsidiary of that name became
active around 2001; we lose some post-2001 IP-holder filings but
eliminate the collision. Readers should treat the firm-mean numbers
below as indicative rather than authoritative; the qualitative pattern
(winners cluster on positive firm-mean $\Delta KL$; failed firms split
across both extremes) is robust to the residual collision rate but
specific decimal places are not.

37 of 41 winners have \emph{positive} firm-mean $\Delta KL$ (the four
exceptions are Adobe ($-0.03$), Qualcomm ($-0.01$), Etsy ($-0.01$),
and Wayfair ($-0.01$)). The winners are not extreme outliers on
distinctiveness: their firm-mean prospective KL is mostly $3.4$--$4.8$
nats, around or just below the corpus median. They use mainstream
vocabulary, but the mainstream vocabulary they use is consistently
the kind the field migrates toward, not away from.

\paragraph{First-real-filing $\Delta KL$ is sharper on a subset.}
Thirteen canonical winners had first-filing $\Delta KL$ above $+0.4$
nats (roughly $2\sigma$): \textbf{Oracle} ($+4.39$, 1977),
\textbf{Intel} ($+3.26$, 1968), \textbf{Microsoft} ($+2.99$, 1975),
\textbf{eBay} ($+1.73$, 1997), \textbf{Apple} ($+1.68$, 1976),
\textbf{Adobe} ($+1.28$, 1982), \textbf{Coinbase} ($+0.98$, 2013),
\textbf{Lyft} ($+0.76$, 2012), \textbf{Twitter} ($+0.75$, 2007),
\textbf{Uber} ($+0.70$, 2009), \textbf{AMD} ($+0.69$, 1969),
\textbf{LinkedIn} ($+0.58$, 2003), and \textbf{Salesforce} ($+0.43$,
1999). Oracle's first filing ($+4.39$) is the largest first-filing
$\Delta KL$ in the curated set; the eBay filing's text ``on-line
trading services in which seller posts items to be auctioned and
bidding is done electronically'' ($+1.73$) introduced phrasing that
downstream filings extensively echoed.

\paragraph{The failed-firm panel does not collapse to negative
$\Delta KL$.} Among 17 widely-recognised failures, the split is
roughly even. \textbf{Webvan} ($-0.22$), \textbf{MoviePass} ($-0.27$),
\textbf{Better.com} ($-0.12$), and \textbf{Pets.com} ($-0.03$) failed
on the receding side. But \textbf{Theranos} ($+0.05$),
\textbf{Beyond Meat} ($+0.34$, first $+0.74$), \textbf{Casper}
($+0.06$, first $+0.46$), \textbf{Solyndra} ($+0.44$), \textbf{Nikola}
($+0.03$, first $+0.24$), and \textbf{Myspace} ($+0.07$) failed
despite trend-setting vocabulary. $\Delta KL$ is a vocabulary signal,
not a business-model signal. \emph{Negative $\Delta KL$ raises the
probability of being on the wrong side of an industry transition.
Positive $\Delta KL$ raises the probability of being on the right
side. Neither is destiny.}

\section{Pioneers, fast-followers, and the late wave}
\label{sec:pioneer-ladder}

To take ``where in the adoption sequence do firms succeed?'' as an
empirical question, for each of five category-defining buzzwords ---
\texttt{website}, \texttt{wireless}, \texttt{online retail},
\texttt{social media}, \texttt{blockchain} --- we ranked every firm in
the corpus by the date of its first filing whose goods/services text
contained the buzzword. We bucket firms by adoption rank
($1$--$10$, $11$--$50$, $51$--$200$, $201$--$1{,}000$,
$1{,}001$--$5{,}000$, $5{,}001+$) and compute, for each bucket, the
survival rate of the firm's first-buzzword-mentioning filing and the
share that appear in SEC EDGAR (a proxy for ``ever publicly traded'').

\paragraph{Pioneer position is bad; the fast-follower band is the
sweet spot.} For \texttt{wireless}, the first $10$ adopters had
\emph{zero} survival of their initial filing; the first $50$ had
$0$--$13\%$. For \texttt{online retail}, the first $10$ survived at
$10\%$ vs.\ $\sim 33\%$ for the late-wave $5{,}000$+ adopters. The
consistent pattern: the very-earliest adopters do badly,
fast-followers (ranks $50$--$1{,}000$) do best, and the mass late wave
($5{,}000$+) is uniformly worst on both survival and IPO rate.
Among public firms in each bucket, the rank-$50$--$1{,}000$ band has
$1.5\!-\!4\times$ the late-entrant baseline IPO rate.

\caption{Adoption-rank bucket vs.\ 5-year survival rate (left of each
pair) and probability that the owner is in SEC EDGAR (right of each

\paragraph{Pioneer-ladder identifies vocabulary prescience, not
firm-builder prescience.} Of $150$ named top-$30$ pioneers across the
five buzzwords, only two had SEC-listed status: \textbf{Sony} (rank 23
on \texttt{wireless}, 1960 filing) and \textbf{PC~Connection} (rank
20 on \texttt{online retail}, 1996). The dominant firms in each
category --- Amazon, Google, eBay, Facebook, Coinbase --- are absent
from the top-30 named pioneer lists because they used product-specific
language in their early filings rather than the category buzzword.
Pioneer-ladder rank identifies \emph{who claimed the
category-defining vocabulary first}, not \emph{who built the dominant
firm in the category}.

\section{Vanishing trends, emerging trends, and the squeeze in between}
\label{sec:flux-tokens}

\paragraph{Concrete vanishing-trend examples.} The
most-negative-$\Delta KL$ filings in the pre-2000 era include
\textsc{Monotype ITC} (1992, $\Delta KL = -2.08$, ``typefaces,

typographical symbols recorded on'') and \textsc{H.\ Berthold
Aktiengesellschaft} (1991, $-1.99$). The desktop-publishing transition
was already destroying the vocabulary of mechanical-and-photo
typesetting in real time. In the 2000s, $\Delta KL$ picks out the

services; restaurant and bar services; making hotel reservations for
others'' --- the language disintermediated by Expedia, Booking.com,
and Airbnb. In the 2010s, the bottom is dominated by the e-cigarette
boom: nearly every extreme-negative-$\Delta KL$ filing in
2014--2015 is ``Electronic cigarette liquid (e-liquid) comprised of
flavorings in liquid form,'' a template the 2016 FDA Deeming Rule and
2020 Premarket Tobacco Application deadline restructured out of the
corpus.

\section{Outcome 1: Trademark survival}
\label{sec:survival}

We derive three binary outcomes per filing from the USPTO status
code. The first two are the ones reported in the figure; the third
appears in the regression table for completeness.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Completed registration:} status code begins 6 or 8.
        The applicant cleared examination, was published for
        opposition, and was issued the registration. Filing-to-
        registration lag is typically nine to twenty-four months.
  \item \textbf{Renewed 5-year registration:} completed registration,
        is at least five years old, and was not cancelled with status
        code 686 (Section-8 maintenance failure). Strict subset of
        completed-registration within any single filing.
  \item \textbf{Renewed 10-year registration:} renewed-5y, is at
        least ten years old, and was not cancelled with status code
        688 (Section-9 renewal failure). Strict subset of renewed-5y.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{How trademark maintenance interacts with these outcomes.}
A registered trademark stays in the live-registered status (6xx)
until either its owner actively surrenders it or one of two
periodic maintenance events comes due and the owner fails to file
the appropriate paperwork. The first event is the Section~8
Declaration of Use, due between the fifth and sixth year after
registration; failure to file it triggers a status-686 cancellation.
The second is the Section~9 Renewal at year ten, with subsequent
renewals every ten years. A firm that goes out of business in,
say, year three of its trademark's life will leave the trademark
in 6xx status for another two-to-three years until Section~8 comes
due and is missed. The 5-year survival outcome thus picks up the
first deliberate ``does this firm still care about this mark?''
gate; we apply a cohort cap of filed $\le 2020$ throughout, so
every clean-panel filing has had time for the Section~8 event to
have played out. The 10-year outcome has not had time to play out
for many filings under that cap and so we report it only with the
caveat that the underlying Section~9 cancellation code is rare
enough to make it nearly indistinguishable from the 5-year measure.

We estimate generalized linear logit models of the form
\[
\Pr(\text{outcome}_i = 1) = \mathrm{logit}^{-1}\!\bigl(\beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot z_i + \gamma_1 \log n_i + \gamma_2 (t_i - \bar t) + \gamma_3 (t_i - \bar t)^2\bigr)
\]
where $z_i$ is a $z$-scored predictor (either $\Delta KL$ alone or
$KL^{\text{pros}}$ and $KL^{\text{retr}}$ jointly), $n_i$ is the count of
distinct dictionary terms in filing $i$, and $t_i$ is the filing year. A
linear-plus-quadratic year control replaces year fixed effects, which
caused separation in the recent cohorts where the outcome rate is
structurally near zero.

$\Delta KL$ has a positive relationship with each survival outcome
in Software \& Electronics ($n = 1{,}423{,}405$), but the regression
coefficient itself is hard to read in log-odds units. The
percentile-based translation is more transparent. Within Class~9, we
sort filings by $\Delta KL$ into deciles. The bottom decile of
$\Delta KL$ (least innovative) reaches registration $48.4\%$ of the
time and survives ten years $35.7\%$ of the time. The top decile
(most innovative) reaches registration $55.5\%$ of the time and
survives ten years \textbf{$41.1\%$} of the time. \textbf{A filing
in the 90th percentile of $\Delta KL$ is roughly $14\%$ more likely
to clear registration and $15\%$ more likely to renew its
registration after a decade than a median filing}, with the
relationship non-monotonic in the middle deciles --- the shape is
broadly U--shaped, with both the very-low and the very-high
$\Delta KL$ filings outperforming the middle. We test the
U--shape formally in Section~\ref{sec:u-shape}.

In log-odds terms, decomposed jointly, prospective surprise (novelty
at filing time, holding retrospective surprise fixed) actually
\emph{penalises} every outcome ($\beta_{\text{pros}} = +0.633$ in
isolation but with retrospective in the model the marginal
contribution flips), while retrospective surprise (the filing
remained unusual even after five years of subsequent filings,
i.e.\ a filing nobody imitated) reverses sign
($\beta_{\text{retr}} = -0.662$). Read together: registration is
\emph{rewarded} for filings whose future resembles them and is
neutral or slightly penalised for filings the future does not come
to resemble. Beer \& Soft Drinks shows the same sign pattern at
smaller magnitude.

\paragraph{What kind of failure is this?}
A trademark filing can fail to register for several distinct reasons,
and they have different theoretical interpretations. Failure to file a
Statement of Use means the applicant filed an intent-to-use mark but
never actually put it in commerce within the 36-month statutory window
--- a corporate decision, not an examiner judgment. Failure to respond
to an office action typically follows an examiner's initial objection
(usually a likelihood-of-confusion citation against an existing
registration); applicant non-response converts the objection into
abandonment. Examiner final refusal is the only mode that is
unambiguously a USPTO judgment that the application cannot proceed.

Table~\ref{tab:failure-modes} decomposes the $50.4\%$ of Class~9
filings that did not reach registration. Two facts dominate. First, the
overwhelming majority ($64\%$) are no-Statement-of-Use abandonments ---
the applicant filed an aspirational ITU mark and never used it.
Second, examiner final refusals account for only $8\%$ of failures.
Across every failure category the mean $\Delta KL$ is within
$0.04$ nats of the registered baseline, which is small compared to the
within-class standard deviation of $\Delta KL$ ($\sigma \approx 0.46$).
Novel filings are not, in any meaningful sense, being rejected.

\section{Outcome 2: Financial success}
\label{sec:financials}

The trademark survival results in Section~\ref{sec:survival} establish
that the innovation axis is meaningful at the per-filing level, but
they say nothing about whether innovation pays the firm a financial
return. To test that, we link USPTO owner names to SEC EDGAR firm
names entirely automatically; \textbf{no entity match in this paper
was reviewed by hand}. The procedure has two passes.

The first pass aggressively normalises both sides --- uppercase,
strip punctuation, drop trailing legal-entity suffixes such as
\texttt{INC}, \texttt{LLC}, \texttt{CORP}, \texttt{HOLDINGS},
\texttt{LIMITED}, \texttt{COMPANY}, \texttt{GMBH}, \texttt{S.A.},
\texttt{KK}, etc. (the full list is in
\texttt{scripts/sec\_link.py})  --- and accepts only exact matches
on the normalised key. The second pass uses fuzzy token-sort
matching (\texttt{rapidfuzz} with a score cutoff of 92 out of 100)
to capture the long tail of near-variants for USPTO owners with at
least 10 filings. The combined crosswalk recovers $14{,}280$ USPTO
owners ($9{,}370$ exact $+\,4{,}910$ fuzzy) covering $627{,}349$
trademark filings ($3.8\%$ of the all-class corpus by filing count,
but a much larger share by economic weight, because matched owners
are by definition publicly-traded firms with material financial
reporting). After joining to SEC firm-year financials and
restricting to firm-years with non-degenerate gross margin and at
least one trademark filing in the prior three years, the analysis
panel contains $5{,}326$ firm-years across $1{,}597$ unique CIKs
over fiscal years 2009--2024.

The fully-automated procedure means the crosswalk has both
false positives (e.g.\ two unrelated firms with similar normalised
names) and false negatives (e.g.\ a public firm filing
trademarks under an unusual subsidiary name that neither pass
matches). The published \texttt{data\_publish/comparators/
external\_crosswalk.csv} file records every match decision so any
reader who wants to override individual entries can do so without
re-running the pipeline.

\paragraph{Filing volume is itself an innovation marker, but also a
size marker.} A firm filing many trademarks in a short window may be
innovating across product lines --- but it may also simply be a large
firm with a large legal department. We separate the two by including
the count of filings as a third predictor alongside the novelty score
itself, and by controlling for log revenue throughout. Both effects
turn out to be present and partially independent.

For each firm-year $(c, y)$ we compute the trailing 3-year mean of the
firm's filing-level scores: $\overline{\Delta KL}_{c, y}$, the
prospective and retrospective components, the maximum within-window
$\Delta KL$ (the firm's most-novel filing in the window), and the
count of filings. We then estimate
\[
y_{c,t} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot z(\text{novelty})_{c,t} + \gamma_1 \log \mathrm{Revenue}_{c,t} + \gamma_2 \mathrm{R\&D~intensity}_{c,t} + \mathrm{year~controls} + \epsilon_{c,t}
\]
with cluster-robust standard errors at the firm level. Outcomes are
gross margin, net margin, and operating margin.

filing count and the maximum within-window $\Delta KL$: both contribute
positively to gross margin ($+3.3$ and $+1.9$ pp/$\sigma$ respectively)
even after controlling for log revenue, suggesting that filing volume
captures something beyond firm size.

\paragraph{A coarser check: does $\Delta KL$ predict reaching SEC EDGAR
at all?}
A firm that files an income statement with the SEC has cleared a
much lower bar of success than ``high gross margin'' --- it has gone
public, registered securities under the '33 Act, or otherwise reached a
reporting threshold. We can therefore re-cast the financial-outcome
question as a binary one: among trademark-filing firms, does $\Delta KL$
predict the probability of \emph{ever} appearing in our SEC crosswalk?
Because the crosswalk's fuzzy-match pass requires owners with
$\geq 10$ filings, we restrict this analysis to the
$160{,}643$ crosswalk-eligible firms with $\geq 10$ clean filings;
$7{,}810$ of them ($4.86\%$) match into SEC EDGAR.

Table~\ref{tab:public-listing} shows the result: $\Delta KL$ has
essentially no relationship with the public-listing rate.
P(ever-public) ranges from $5.11\%$ in the most-tired quintile (Q1) to
$4.44\%$ in the most-innovative quintile (Q5), with the middle three
quintiles all near $5\%$. A logistic regression of \texttt{ever\_public}
on $z(\overline{\Delta KL})$ and $\log_{10}(\text{n\_filings})$ confirms
this pattern: the $\Delta KL$ coefficient is $-0.019$ with SE $0.013$
($p = 0.14$, indistinguishable from zero), while
$\log_{10}(\text{n\_filings})$ carries a coefficient of $+1.578$
(SE $0.030$, $p < 10^{-9}$). A 10$\times$ increase in filing count
multiplies the odds of going public by $\sim$$4.85$; a one-SD increase
in mean $\Delta KL$ multiplies them by $\sim$$0.98$.

\paragraph{Conditional on public listing: trademark count predicts
revenue but not margin.}
A natural follow-up: among the firms that did IPO, does trademark
count correlate with firm value? The patent-economics literature
finds that count and value move together --- prolific patenters
have valuable patents (Trajtenberg 1990; Hall, Jaffe \& Trajtenberg
2005, \emph{RAND J.\ Econ.}) --- and we wondered whether the
opposite might hold for trademarks, where a smaller portfolio could
be evidence of operational focus. On the SEC-crosswalked panel

\emph{strongly positively} correlated with firm size and
\emph{essentially uncorrelated} with margin

decile (median $1$--$2$ marks), but its mean gross margin is
essentially identical. The focus hypothesis is rejected on size,
absent on profitability, and weakly supported only on R\&D
intensity (firms with many marks tend to file less R\&D against
their revenue --- a different culture, not a strategic focus
signal).

\section{Outcome 3: Debut-filing-only test}
\label{sec:debut}

The pooled per-filing logit in Section~\ref{sec:survival} treats each of
a firm's filings as an independent observation, and the firm-year
panel in Section~\ref{sec:financials} averages each firm's recent
trademark portfolio. Both invite the criticism that the apparent
``novelty pays'' result might be reading filer sophistication or past
financial slack rather than the novelty of any specific filing. The
sharpest test we can run on this data is to restrict the analysis to
each registrant's first-ever filing in the corpus: a firm cannot have
funded its debut filing with the proceeds of its earlier filings,
because there were none.

We collect, for every owner name appearing in any of the 45 NICE
classes, the row corresponding to the earliest filing date associated
with that owner. The result is $2{,}891{,}725$ debut filings (one per
owner) eligible under the standard 1995--2020 cohort and reference-window
restrictions. Joining to the SEC crosswalk gives $11{,}914$ debut
filings whose owner matches a publicly-traded firm; restricting to firms
with at least two years of subsequent SEC financial reporting yields a
firm-debut financial panel of $6{,}525$ firms.

\input{results/first_filing_table.tex}

The pattern is consistent and informative.

\textbf{Survival.} On debut filings, the $\Delta KL$ coefficient on
completing registration shrinks from the pooled value of $+0.124$ to
\textbf{$-0.034$} (still highly significant on $2.9$M observations). A
debut filer who introduces unusually novel goods/services language
appears to face slightly more registration friction --- the opposite
sign of the pooled effect. The pooled positive coefficient was reading
filer sophistication: firms with deep filing portfolios know how to
write a description that survives examination, and they also tend to
file more novel marks. Among first-time filers neither advantage is
present. Renewal coefficients are smaller in magnitude but stay
positive ($+0.042$ at the 10y mark), suggesting that long-run
persistence still favours novelty among debut filings.

\paragraph{Are debut filings less novel than later filings?} The negative
debut-registration coefficient invites a natural worry: if first filings
were systematically less novel, then perhaps the apparent ``debut
novelty pays'' result is an artefact of restricted variance.
This is not what the data show. On the same all-class clean panel,
mean $\Delta KL$ on debut filings is $+0.052$ (median $+0.008$), versus
$+0.010$ (median $-0.010$) on non-debut filings; the share of filings
with positive $\Delta KL$ is $51.5\%$ on debuts and $47.6\%$ on
non-debuts. Both component KLs are larger on debuts (prospective $5.22$
vs.\ $4.91$, retrospective $5.17$ vs.\ $4.90$): debut filings read as
less like the recent past \emph{and} less like the near future. The
intuition is that a firm's debut is often a stake in genuinely new
territory, while its later filings drift toward category-conventional
language. So the negative registration coefficient on debuts is not
explained by lower novelty in the debut set --- if anything debuts are
slightly more novel; it is explained by examination friction. A
first-time filer with unusual goods/services language draws office
actions and lacks the procedural sophistication to navigate them, while
an established repeat filer with the same novel language clears the
process routinely. The pooled positive coefficient was reading the
established-firm advantage; stripping it leaves a small examination-
friction penalty for first-time filers writing in non-standard ways.

Within firms, the debut filing sits roughly at the firm's own median
novelty (mean within-firm percentile $0.514$ on $432{,}180$ firms with
$\geq 5$ clean filings), but the distribution is bimodal:
$24.4\%$ of firms file their debut in the bottom $20\%$ of their own
$\Delta KL$ distribution (cautious entrants), and $30.4\%$ file their
debut in the top $20\%$ (bold entrants); the middle three quintiles each
account for only $14$--$16\%$. Few firms enter at mid-novelty. And among
multi-filing firms, $53\%$ of \emph{non-debut} filings have $\Delta KL <
0$ — a slight tilt toward line-extension language, although the per-firm
mix is heterogeneous: about a fifth of firms are persistently novel
(at most $20\%$ of their non-debuts in tired territory) and another
quarter are persistently tired ($\geq 80\%$).

\textbf{Financial.} On the firm-debut panel, a one-standard-deviation
increase in the firm's debut-filing $\Delta KL$ is associated with
\textbf{$+1.7$ percentage points} higher mean future gross margin (SE
$0.3$, $n = 6{,}525$ firms) and $+0.5$ percentage points higher
within-firm margin standard deviation (SE $0.15$, $n = 5{,}008$
firms). Both effects are roughly half the magnitude of the pooled
trailing-3-year results in Section~\ref{sec:financials}. We take the
attenuation as evidence that part of the original firm-year reward
reflects the habit/portfolio confound flagged in the previous section,
but that a substantive debut effect
remains: the novelty of a firm's first trademark filing predicts its
subsequent average financial performance, even before the firm has
produced any other trademark filings to ``fund'' the bet.

\subsection*{Debut $\Delta KL$ predicts going public; line-extension
filings reverse the appearance}
\label{sec:debut-ipo}

\emph{At the firm-debut level, positive $\Delta KL$ raises the
probability of ever going public.} Restricting to each owner's
first-ever filing across all classes ($n = 5{,}090{,}053$ in the
clean H=2y panel), $P(\text{owner ever in SEC EDGAR})$ by debut
$\Delta KL$ bin is \emph{monotone increasing}: $0.42\%$ at the
bottom decile, $0.47\%$ in the middle, $0.64\%$ at the top decile ---
a $+52\%$ relative lift for positive-$\Delta KL$ debut filings over
negative-$\Delta KL$ debut filings (Figure~\ref{fig:debut-outcome-by-kl}
panel~C). On the firm's first move, having a vocabulary that the
field subsequently echoes predicts becoming a publicly-reported
firm.

The same regression at the full-corpus filing level shows the
\emph{opposite} direction, and a reader who saw only that result
would draw the wrong conclusion. At the filing level,
$P(\text{owner ever in SEC EDGAR})$ \emph{decreases} monotonically
with $\Delta KL$: the bottom decile is $5.8\%$ public, the top decile
$3.5\%$. The reversal is mechanical. Public-firm owners ---
incumbents like Apple, Coca-Cola, Procter \& Gamble, Pfizer --- file
many trademarks per year, and the bulk of an incumbent's portfolio
is line extensions that reuse the firm's established (low-$\Delta KL$)
vocabulary. Their volume swamps the per-filing statistic and
concentrates the public-firm mass in the low-$\Delta KL$ bin. The
right unit of analysis for the ``do innovative firms reach the public
market?'' question is the firm's debut filing; the full-corpus
filing-level statistic is dominated by post-IPO line-extension
activity.

For prospective and retrospective KL on debut filings,
$P(\text{public})$ is monotone \emph{decreasing}: the
very-distinctive-on-day-one debut firms have lower IPO rates
($0.31\%$--$0.34\%$ at the top decile) than the
average-distinctiveness debut firms ($0.51\%$ overall baseline).
Niche on day one tends to mean niche at maturity; the IPO sweet
spot for debut firms is ordinary vocabulary used in a forward-drifting
direction --- low PC1, high PC2 in the decomposition.

\paragraph{Both tails are saturating exponentials in $|\Delta KL|$
--- two distinct theories made visible.}
The survival lift at the extremes is most diagnostic on debut
filings, where there are no within-firm portfolio confounds. On the
debut-only panel ($n = 5{,}088{,}700$ owner-first filings), we fit a
saturating exponential of survival on $|\Delta KL|$ separately on
each side:
\[
P(\text{survived 5y}) \;=\; a + b\,e^{-c\,|\Delta KL|}.
\]

\emph{Innovation side ($\Delta KL > 0$): optimal distinctiveness as
a saturating exponential.} On the positive side ($n = 2{,}502{,}574$)
the fit is $a = 0.995$, $b = -0.55$, $c = 0.063$ with $R^2 = 0.499$
across $20$ bins. The curve is monotone increasing in $\Delta KL$
and concave-down: an additional unit of prospective-novelty pays at
a decreasing rate. This is the empirical content of optimal
distinctiveness (Deephouse 1999; Zhao, Fisher, Lounsbury \& Miller
2017) translated from level-of-distinctiveness to directional
vocabulary flux. The strict inverted-$U$ prediction (that survival
turns down at high distinctiveness) is not what we observe; the
underlying empirical claim (that distinctiveness has positive but
saturating returns) is supported and shows up in an explicit
functional form. We treat this as a result, not a refutation: the
optimal-distinctiveness literature's measurement instrument was the
\emph{level} of category-typicality, ours is the \emph{change} in
category-typicality, and the saturating-exponential response is the
form that the underlying claim takes when restated on a
directional-flux axis.

\emph{Harvest side ($\Delta KL < 0$): increasing returns to
late-stage imitation.} On the negative side ($n = 2{,}586{,}126$)
the same functional form fits much more tightly: $a = 0.573$, $b =
-0.137$, $c = 3.33$, $R^2 = 0.978$ across $20$ bins. Conditional on
negative $\Delta KL$ the Pearson correlation between $|\Delta KL|$
and survival is $+0.054$ (Spearman $+0.058$). The substantive
interpretation: filings whose vocabulary lies further into the
receding/legacy side of the category are not penalised but rewarded,
and the reward \emph{grows} the deeper the harvest. The asymptote
($a = 0.573$, close to the corpus baseline) is the ceiling: the
late-stage-imitation niche has a finite size, and harvest-tail
filings approach the niche-saturation survival rate quickly
($c = 3.33$ vs.\ $c = 0.063$ on the innovation side). The
small-firm-can-pick-up-the-legacy-niche reading is that few firms
stay in legacy vocabulary; the ones who do compete against few
peers, and the marginal return to going even deeper is high but
capped by the size of the legacy niche itself.

\paragraph{Category-killer confound: explicitly considered.} One
mechanism we cannot fully rule out in principle is that a category's
vocabulary recedes in the corpus precisely because a single dominant
firm has captured the category --- ``Xerox'' becomes the only
trademark using \texttt{xerographic} not because the niche is dying
but because every other firm has been pushed out by the
category-killer. If this were the mechanism, the harvest tail would
not be ``firms doing well in a receding niche'' but ``firms with the
only surviving trademark in a niche they killed for everyone else.''
We treat this as implausible at the scale we observe for three
reasons. First, the $n = 2{,}586{,}126$ harvest-side debut filings
span $44$ industries with no industry dominated by a single firm to
that degree; the harvest tail is broad, not concentrated in a
handful of category-killer owners. Second, the token-level evidence
(\texttt{microfiche}, \texttt{dial-up}, \texttt{pager},
\texttt{store-and-forward}, \texttt{lager}, \texttt{stout},
\texttt{shandy}, \texttt{annuity}, \texttt{microcomputers}) shows
that the receding vocabulary is genuinely receding in the corpus
across many filers, not just in one or two firms' portfolios.
Third, on the time window we study ($1990$--$2021$), the
fast-fashion of trademark categories does not include classic
category-killer episodes (Xerox/xerographic, Kleenex/tissue) which
are mid-20th-century phenomena; the harvest-tail tokens we see are
sunsetting under platform shifts and regulatory transitions, not
under monopolistic genericisation by an individual firm. Worth
flagging, not worth treating as a competing explanation.

\section{Stock-market returns}
\label{sec:returns}

strongest test is whether it predicts subsequent stock-market returns
above a market benchmark. We pulled monthly adjusted-close prices
from Yahoo Finance for every CIK in the USPTO--SEC crosswalk that

penny-stock-spike outliers; cluster-robust standard errors at the

firm-year mean across that year's filings, and debut-only.

one year, $+19.2$ pp at three years, and peaks at \textbf{$+28.4$
pp} at four years before partially mean-reverting to $+14.9$ pp at
five years. Both specifications are statistically significant at the
one-year horizon and grow more so at intermediate horizons. The
debut specification eliminates the past-success-funds-current-novelty
confound and still produces the largest excess-return coefficient in
the paper. The five-year mean-reversion is consistent with a story
in which innovative firms enjoy a multi-year ``imitated category
leader'' window before competition catches up.

\paragraph{Surprise alone does not predict returns.}
A natural variant of the regression uses prospective KL alone as
the predictor (rather than the $\Delta KL$ composite). Doing so
eliminates the result entirely: at the firm-year level the
prospective-only coefficient is essentially zero at every horizon
($+0.001$ at 1y, $-0.001$ at 5y); at the debut level it is also
zero with wider standard errors ($+0.001$ at 1y, $+0.057$ at 5y,
both indistinguishable from null). \textbf{Mere novelty at filing
time does not predict subsequent stock returns. Only novelty
that the future ends up imitating does.} The retrospective
component is itself a forward-looking signal of which novel filings
turned out to define a category, and the predictive content of
$\Delta KL$ for returns sits in that selection.

\paragraph{Patents and trademarks index different innovation
constructs.} The growing-with-horizon pattern on patents is what we
would expect if patent counts indexed \emph{continuing} innovation
activity: a firm with many recent patents is one whose R\&D pipeline
is still producing, and the future cash flows of that pipeline are
priced in increasingly across two-, three-, and four-year-ahead
returns. The trademark $\Delta KL$ signal is qualitatively
different. The debut-only specification --- one observation per firm,
keyed on the firm's first-ever trademark filing --- treats $\Delta KL$
as a \emph{one-time positional bet}: did the founding team pick a
vocabulary the field would migrate toward? The horizon profile of
the debut $\Delta KL$ coefficient (rising to $+28$ pp at four years
and then shrinking) is consistent with a one-time bet whose
realisation takes a few years to be priced and then becomes
information the market has already absorbed. The two innovation
proxies are not redundant (the $R^2$ decomposition below confirms
sub-additivity) and they answer different questions. Patents proxy
\emph{whether the firm is still innovating}; $\Delta KL$ on the
firm's debut filing proxies \emph{whether the firm started in
fertile linguistic ground}.

\paragraph{Are patents and trademark $\Delta KL$ complementary or
redundant? An $R^2$ decomposition.}
The complement-vs-substitute question is not rhetorical --- we can
compute the answer. Restricting to the common panel of firm-years
in which we observe \emph{both} a patent count and a trademark
$\Delta KL$ (and the firm appears in our SEC returns data), we run
eight nested OLS specifications on the same panel of $7{,}607$
firm-years and report the $R^2$ of each (controls in every spec are
year linear and quadratic). We add a third predictor, $\log(1+
\text{patents})$ (P) and the trademark composite $\Delta KL$ (I, for
``innovator''), but also \emph{novelty alone} (S, the prospective
KL component) so that we can separately ask whether plain
filing-time surprise contributes anything.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Cumulative $R^2$ from each combination of predictors on
excess return at horizon $k$, on the common panel of firm-years
with both a patent count and a trademark KL score. All predictors
$z$-scored; year linear and quadratic controls in every cell;
cluster-robust SEs at the firm level (suppressed). The marginal
contribution of each predictor is the difference from the
controls-only baseline.}
\label{tab:r2-decomp}
\input{results/r2_decomposition.tex}
\end{table}

Three patterns are visible in Table~\ref{tab:r2-decomp}.

\textbf{(1) All $R^2$ values are small.} On a common panel of
$7{,}607$ firm-years, controls (year and year-squared) explain
between $0.7\%$ and $3.4\%$ of cumulative-excess-return variance.
This is what one expects from firm-level returns: most of the
variance is idiosyncratic. The contribution of any innovation
proxy is necessarily a small slice of an already-small explainable
share.

\textbf{(2) Patents and innovator $\Delta KL$ are very nearly
additive.} Patents alone add $+0.06$ to $+0.23$ pp of $R^2$ above
controls (growing with horizon); innovator $\Delta KL$ alone adds
$+0.10$ to $+0.20$ pp (peaking at three years). Their joint
contribution P + I is $+0.16$ to $+0.33$ pp, essentially the sum of
the individual contributions. The two measures share almost no
explanatory variance for stock returns; they pick out different
firms, and each firm-feature carries independent return information.

\textbf{(3) Novelty (prospective KL) alone adds essentially nothing.}
The S-only specification is statistically indistinguishable from
controls at every horizon ($+0.00$ to $+0.01$ pp), and the marginal
$R^2$ of S in the P+I+S spec relative to P+I is $\le 0.01$ pp at
every horizon. The result we documented earlier --- that a filing
that is merely surprising at the time, without later imitation, does
not predict returns --- is reproduced cleanly in the $R^2$
decomposition. The forward-looking component
($-KL_{\text{retr}}$) is what makes $\Delta KL$ a return predictor,
not the backward-looking surprise itself.

We therefore replace the previous sentence ``the two measures appear
to be complements'' with the more precise statement: \textbf{patents
and trademark $\Delta KL$ are quantitatively additive on stock
returns; their joint $R^2$ contribution is the sum of the marginal
contributions of each in isolation, and novelty alone adds nothing.}
A firm's patent count and a firm's trademark $\Delta KL$ are two
substantively different innovation channels and the predictive
content of one is not contained in the other.

\paragraph{What is a trademark really protecting?}
The convergent-validity result invites a second look at what the
trademark filing fundamentally is. A trademark is a legal monopoly
over a particular set of words, designs, or sounds in connection
with a particular set of goods and services. The economic value of
that monopoly comes from how distinctive the goods/services
description actually is: a generic mark on a generic product
(\texttt{Beer} for an ale) is hard to defend and easy for competitors
to crowd; a distinctive description for a genuinely new offering
(\texttt{Hard Seltzer Works} for a new beverage category) keeps
later entrants from advertising in the same space without infringing.
Read this way, the goods/services text is not just a description of
the firm; it is a legal carve-out around the firm's claim to a
particular novelty. A trademark filing high in $\Delta KL$ is, in
this framing, an attempt to reserve the language of a market
offering before the rest of the industry has named it. That the
filing later gets imitated --- the empirical mark of a high-$\Delta KL$
mark --- is exactly the substantive event a successful trademark
exists to police. The financial reward we measure is, in part, the
value of having claimed the boundary of a market category before
competitors arrived to share it.

\section{Templating and survival: do shared descriptions predict failure?}
\label{sec:templated-survival}

The diagonal-clustering geometry of the topomap suggests that firms
filing in the lower-left ``commonplace'' corner --- low prospective
\emph{and} low retrospective surprise --- have the worst
post-registration survival. A natural mechanism would be templated
copy-paste descriptions: filers using boilerplate that examiners and
the marketplace alike learn to discount. We test this directly. For
every clean filing, we compute the count of \emph{other} filings
sharing the exact same lowercased, punctuation-stripped goods/services
text, then bin filings by that multiplicity and report registration
and post-registration-survival rates.

The result is the opposite of the templated-failure hypothesis.

\paragraph{Robustness: dropping the $n_{\text{terms}} \geq 3$ filter.}

(Table~\ref{tab:clean-filter}), which excludes 1- and 2-token G/S
descriptions because their per-filing $KL$ is degenerate. Many of the
most-templated strings are short --- ``restaurant services'',
``wines'', ``footwear'', ``t shirts'', ``candy'', ``cosmetics'',
``jewelry'' --- and so are dropped from the headline panel even when
the analysis at hand does not depend on $KL$ at all. To check that
the templating-survival pattern is not an artefact of this filter, we
re-run the analysis on \textsc{clean}--without--$n_{\text{terms}}$
($8{,}499{,}455$ filings, $+591$k vs.\ headline). The pattern is
nearly identical for the $1$- through $1001$--$5000$ bins ($\leq 1$
percentage point of drift on either rate). The only meaningful change
is the $5000+$ bin: still-live rate falls from $49.2\%$ to $44.2\%$,
because the short commodity templates that the headline filter
excludes survive at corpus-average rates rather than the elevated
rates of the longer structured templates that dominate the
$n_{\text{terms}}$-filtered $5000+$ bin (e.g.\ ``athletic apparel
namely shirts pants jackets footwear hats and caps athletic
uniforms,'' $13{,}047$ copies, $61.6\%$ still-live).

\paragraph{So why is the lower-left of the topomap so bad?}
The diagonal-clustering reading from the topomap survives this audit:
the worst-survival cell is firms whose filings cluster on the
diagonal at low $KL$. But the mechanism is not ``templated text gets
penalised.'' Quite the reverse --- explicitly templated text helps.
What appears to drive the lower-left corner is one-off, generic G/S
text that nobody else copies verbatim: descriptions written in the
class's modal vocabulary but unique enough to count as zero-template,
filed by firms without the procedural sophistication to use the
known-good phrasings their lawyers would otherwise re-use. Boilerplate,
when it exists, signals professional repetition; the bottom-left
cluster is filled with filers who lack the boilerplate.

\section{Both extremes outperform: a U-shape across industries}
\label{sec:u-shape}

The decile-rate plot referenced in Section~\ref{sec:survival} shows
that the relationship between $\Delta KL$ and survival is not
linear; it is U--shaped, with both the most-conservative-debut
filings (lowest decile of $\Delta KL$) and the most-innovative
filings (highest decile) outperforming the middle deciles. The
intuition is that both directions of distance from the modal
filing are rewarded: very-novel filings benefit from category
creation and the highest decile of $\Delta KL$ is dominated by
firms that the future imitated; very-conservative filings benefit
from being unambiguously defensible and easy for the examiner to
parse. Filings in the middle, neither novel enough to claim a new
category nor templated enough to coast on legal-clarity grounds,
fare the worst.

\paragraph{Quadratic regression.}
We test the curvature directly. For every industry's clean
filings between 1990 and 2020, we fit a logistic model with both
$z(\Delta KL)$ and $z(\Delta KL)^2$ as predictors, with year and
year-squared controls. Pooled across all 45 NICE classes and
$9.3$ million filings, the aggregate quadratic coefficient is
positive but small ($\beta_{(\Delta KL)^2}^z = +0.0002$ on 5y
survival; $+0.0013$ on 10y survival). The aggregate pooled
quadratic is not the primary evidence for the U-shape; the
per-industry evidence is. At the per-industry level the quadratic
is positive in $27$ of the $44$ industries we can fit.

\centering

\input{results/u_shape_table.tex}

\paragraph{The U-shape is present in most --- but not all ---
industries.} Fitting the quadratic per industry on the 5y-survival
outcome \emph{pooled across all years} ($n = 44$ industries with at
least $5{,}000$ clean filings), the quadratic coefficient on
$z(\Delta KL)^2$ is positive in $27$ of $44$ industries ($61\%$) and
individually significant at $p < 0.05$ in $21$ of those $44$ ($48\%$).
The U-shape dominates in consumer-goods classes (Household Utensils,
Furniture, Leather, Textiles, Common Metals, etc.), where heavy
templated filing by large incumbents and distinct novelty waves
coexist. Several large information-economy classes show the opposite
on the pooled-years fit, an apparent \emph{inverted-U}: Software \&
Electronics ($t = -18.2$), Insurance \& Financial ($t = -13.8$),
Scientific \& Tech Services ($t = -12.9$), Advertising \& Retail
($t = -10.7$), Telecommunications ($t = -10.6$).

\paragraph{Per-(industry $\times$ year) refinement.} The pooled-years
fit hides whether an industry is consistently $+U$, consistently
inverted-$U$, or shifting between regimes. We re-fit the quadratic
logit separately for every (industry, year) cell ($44$ industries

\paragraph{Is the U driven by extreme-KL filings?}
A natural concern is that the U-shape is generated by a few very
extreme filings on either KL axis --- the most-similar-to-past
filings on the left, the most-imitated filings on the right ---
rather than by a smooth concave-up relationship across the
distribution. We test this by re-fitting the pooled quadratic on
the same outcome after dropping different combinations of the
extremes.

\centering

\label{tab:u-shape-drilldown}
\input{results/u_shape_drilldown.tex}
\end{table}

The result is unambiguous: \textbf{the U-shape does not depend on
extreme-KL filings.} Dropping the bottom $5\%$ of prospective KL
(filings most similar to the past) leaves the quadratic
coefficient at $+0.0082$ ($t = +38.6$), down from $+0.0087$
($t = +41.9$) on the full sample. Dropping the bottom $5\%$ of
retrospective KL (most-similar-to-future filings) leaves it at
$+0.0085$ ($t = +39.9$). Dropping both: $+0.0082$ ($t = +38.2$).
Most strikingly, dropping the bottom and top $10\%$ of $\Delta KL$
\emph{itself} --- i.e., excising the two arms of the U --- yields
a residual quadratic coefficient of $+0.0343$ ($t = +48.7$),
\emph{four times larger} than the full-sample coefficient. The
curvature is intrinsic to the central $80\%$ of the $\Delta KL$
distribution. The U is not driven by the most-templated nor by
the most-novel filings; it is the relationship between novelty and
survival across the entire range.

\paragraph{Two-sided exponential fit.}
A separate test of the same shape is to split the data at
$\Delta KL = 0$ and ask whether the rate rises exponentially with
distance from zero on each side: $\log(\text{rate}) = a + b \cdot
|\Delta KL|$ separately for the negative-$\Delta KL$ and
positive-$\Delta KL$ halves. The pooled aggregate fits, on the
5y-survival outcome, give slopes $b_{\text{neg}} = +0.04$ and
$b_{\text{pos}} = +0.05$ on the bin-aggregated data, both with
$R^2 > 0.7$ across the deciles within each side. Both sides slope
positively in $|\Delta KL|$: each one-nat increase in distance
from zero (in either direction) is associated with roughly a
$4$-to-$5\%$ relative increase in the per-bin renewal rate.

\paragraph{What is consistent with this story?} A natural reach
is to say that the most-imitated novel filings are category
leaders the industry follows and the most-conventional filings
are templated marks that examiners and competitors leave alone.
But that reading credits both tails with prescient strategy ---
the right-arm filers ``saw'' the future their wording would
define, and the left-arm filers ``saw'' that the safest path
was to use the existing canonical wording. Without behavioural
data we cannot license either claim. A more parsimonious story
is that we observe the empirical fact (both extremes outperform
the middle) without needing to ascribe foresight to either tail.
The structural mechanism we \emph{can} document is examiner
heuristics --- the office-action evidence below ---  which gives
both tails an advantage at the registration gate without
requiring any awareness of what the future would do.

\paragraph{An office-action mechanism for the U.}
A more mechanical reading of the same shape comes from
likelihood-of-confusion. The status code 700 ``abandoned, no
response to office action'' is the modal post-examination
failure, and its share by $\Delta KL$ decile is itself U-shaped
in reverse (D1: $5.1\%$; middle deciles 3--5: $13.2$--$14.1\%$;
D10: $9.1\%$). Filings whose wording uses common, well-defined
mid-frequency vocabulary that happens to overlap with prior
registrations get examiner objections; filings with very narrowly
common vocabulary (the bottom decile) and filings with vocabulary
nobody else has used (the top decile) avoid these conflicts.
This mechanism does not require firms in either tail to have
strategic awareness of their own position --- it is a property
of the examiner's prior-art search, mediated by how distinctive
or how generic the goods/services description is. \textbf{We
flag this as the cleanest available mechanism precisely because
it does not ask us to credit the negative-$\Delta KL$ tail with
prescience that we would not also extend to the positive tail.}

\paragraph{We do not have a mechanism story commensurate with the
magnitude of the effect.}
We want to flag this directly. The U-shape on $\Delta KL$ is
empirically robust --- larger than the linear $\Delta KL$ effect we
report on gross margin, present in $27$ of $44$ industries, present
at the filing level even within firms that demonstrably abandoned
some marks and kept others alive, and present at the token level for
both extremes. But \emph{the magnitude of the survival lift at both
flux extremes is greater than any single mechanism we can articulate
explains}. A 5--10 percentage-point gap between flux-neutral and
flux-extreme filings, sustained across millions of filings and
dozens of industries, is not what we expected to find, and the
candidate mechanisms we can name (likelihood-of-confusion examination
artefacts, real-options harvest by incumbents, Schumpeterian creation
at the trend-leading edge) each predict only part of the curve and
none predicts the depth. We document the empirical pattern and
present what falsification we can; we do not claim a unified
mechanism story.

\paragraph{Falsification battery (summary).}
The U-shape is unusual enough to warrant a battery of robustness
checks. Table~\ref{tab:alternatives} reports re-fits of the pooled
survival quadratic on the candidate confounds in summary form;
Appendix~\ref{app:falsification} narrates each check in full. The
quadratic coefficient $\beta_{\Delta KL^2}^z$ stays strongly
positive on every survival sub-sample, with a single exception:
the firm-year gross-margin OLS, where the relationship is roughly
linear in $\Delta KL$ rather than U-shaped. Survival is U; margin
is linear.

The ten checks (Table~\ref{tab:alternatives}, with the
firm-persistence, temporal-stability, competition-density, and
external-corpus tests added) are:
(1) Foreign-extension hypothesis (Madrid-Protocol marks could inflate
the low-$\Delta KL$ tail): \textbf{rejected}; US-only and
foreign-only sub-samples give nearly identical quadratics
($+0.0078$ vs.\ $+0.0100$).
(2) First-time vs.\ recurring filings: \textbf{both have the U},
recurring slightly stronger.
(3) Financial outcomes: \textbf{the U does not appear} in firm-year
gross margin (the relationship is linear); the U lives in survival.
(4) Survivor bias from large incumbents: \textbf{partial} support
for the left arm --- the bottom $\Delta KL$ decile has larger firm
filing-volumes than the middle.
(5) ``Liquid Death'' hypothesis (low-$\Delta KL$ might be novel
products in old vocabulary): \textbf{rejected} on text-length
splits.
(6) Filing-level vs.\ firm-level: \textbf{filing-level} --- the U
survives within firms that abandoned some marks and kept others
alive (Figure~\ref{fig:firm-persistence}).
(7) The blockchain / cryptocurrency / NFT / web3 wave as a possible
imitated-but-failed counterexample: \textbf{not a counterexample}
--- the crypto cohort outperforms on every survival margin.
(8) Temporal stability: \textbf{the U is present in most individual
years, not just in pooled data}. Per-year fits 1990--2021 ($n = 32$
cohorts) show the pooled quadratic is significantly positive in
$23 / 32$ years (Figure~\ref{fig:u-shape-temporal}), independently of
macro cycle. The pooled-across-years result is the average of mostly
year-by-year positive coefficients, not an aggregation artefact.
(9) Competition-density mediation: \textbf{not a confound, but a
mediator}. On 10{,}000 stratified Class-9 focal filings we count
near-competitors (cosine $\geq 0.7$ on g/s TF-IDF) within an
asymmetric hard window $[t-2y,\, t+5y]$ of the focal's filing date
(no decay weighting; this window is for the cosine-neighbour search
only and is distinct from the H=2y exponential-decay reference used
for the KL surprise itself --- a separate object). The pre-window of
2y captures established competitors at filing-time; the post-window
of 5y matches the focal filing's 5y survival horizon (i.e.\ peers
that could plausibly have shaped or been shaped by the focal in its
formative window). We count how many such peers survived 5y from
their own filing date. Flux extremes are in clusters with \emph{more}
surviving peers, not fewer; the flux-neutral middle has the fewest
live peers. Adding $\log(1 + \text{live\_competitors})$ as a control
absorbs $84\%$ of the $\Delta KL$ quadratic on survival
(Figure~\ref{fig:competition-density}).
The U is therefore mediated by niche viability: filings at flux
extremes sit in vocabulary clusters where peers also survive, and the
$\Delta KL$ signal is partly a proxy for which clusters can support real
businesses. The linear $z(\Delta KL)$ effect remains positive and
significant after the mediator is included ($z = +2.65$), so flux is
not fully redundant with cluster viability.
(10) External-corpus confound (Google Books n-grams): \textbf{partial
shared signal, but not redundant}. For 75 extreme-flux tokens (25 per
stratum) we pulled English-language frequency time series from Google
Books 1985--2021 and compared each token's per-year trademark-corpus
frequency to its per-year Google Books frequency.
Per-token Pearson correlation between trademark log-lift and Google
Books log-lift is $+0.47$ (Spearman $+0.39$, $n = 53$ tokens). About
$22\%$ of trademark-flux variance per token is shared with general
English-language usage; the remaining $78\%$ is corpus-specific. The
trademark signal is partly but not wholly a reflection of broader
linguistic drift.

\paragraph{What is sensitive to methodology: per-industry rankings.
What is robust: the per-year U.} A reader who reproduces this analysis
from an earlier draft of the pipeline (with a flat 5-year reference
window rather than the production H=2y exponential decay) will get
visibly different per-industry quadratic coefficients --- enough that
the rank order of industries by U-strength can rearrange, and even
the sign of an individual industry's coefficient can change. We
flag this explicitly. The \emph{per-industry rank} is a sensitive
statistic that depends on how the reference distribution is built; we
would not stake a finding on the precise sign of any single industry's
quadratic. The \emph{per-year} robustness check
(Figure~\ref{fig:u-shape-temporal}) is the appropriate falsification
test for the headline U: if the pooled U were a few-cohort artefact,
it would not survive year-by-year. It does. The temporal-stability
evidence is what we lean on when claiming robustness; the
per-industry ranking is descriptive cross-sectional context, not the
primary evidence.

\paragraph{What else could be going on?}
A handful of further candidate stories that the data so far
are consistent with but cannot directly distinguish:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Narrative vs.\ analytical decision-making.} Firms
        in the middle of the $\Delta KL$ distribution may be
        chasing trends without committing either to the trend's
        canonical vocabulary or to a clearly differentiated
        wording. The data are consistent with this --- the
        worst-performing filings are exactly those that ``follow
        the herd without standing out from it'' --- but we have
        no behavioural data to confirm decision style.
  \item \emph{Pay-phones-after-cell-phones.} The middle deciles
        may include filings whose entrepreneurs are starting
        businesses on templates the industry is in the process
        of abandoning --- not low-novelty enough to coast on
        legal clarity, not high-novelty enough to claim a new
        category. The 1995 Beer \& Soft Drinks trough
        (Section~\ref{sec:u-shape}) is exactly such a cohort.
  \item \emph{Filing volume as a hedge.} Large incumbents file
        many marks per year and accept that some will fail;
        their templated bottom-decile filings benefit from the
        firm's resources for renewal and maintenance even when
        the underlying product fails. The mean firm filing-volume
        by decile evidence above is consistent.
  \item \emph{Examiner heuristics.} Examiners may grant clear
        templates (boilerplate is easy to evaluate) and clear
        novelties (no prior conflict to cite) at higher rates
        than middle-of-the-distribution filings, regardless of
        any underlying business outcome. The status-700 share
        evidence above is consistent.
\end{itemize}

We cannot, with this data, single out which of these mechanisms
dominates. What we can say is that the survival U-shape is
robust to the obvious confounds, that it does not appear in the
firm-year financial outcome (which is roughly linear in $\Delta
KL$), and that there is direct mechanical evidence
(office-action incidence) that middle-$\Delta KL$ filings face
more friction than either tail.

\paragraph{Did Beer \& Soft Drinks go in a failed direction
around 1995?} The notable trough in the Class~32 time-series
near 1995 (Figure~\ref{fig:ts_bev}) is consistent with this
story. Inspecting the lowest-$\Delta KL$ Class~32 filings of
1995 reveals a heavy concentration of \emph{New Age beverage}
brands: Clearly Canadian (\textsc{Tropical Tangerine},
\textsc{Clearly Sparkling Tea}), Koala Springs International
(\textsc{Aussie Slammer}, \textsc{Aussie Fruit Sparkler}), Royal
Crown's instant-drink mixes (\textsc{Yupii},
\textsc{Fruitii Instant Drink}), Empire State Beverage's
flavoured lemonades and teas (\textsc{Sahara Pink Lemonade},
\textsc{Rosewater Lemonade}, \textsc{Moonlight Tea}), and
Nestl\'e's exotic-fruit ginger drinks. This is the
mid-1990s clear-cola / sparkling-tea / iced-tea / exotic-fruit
wave that crested in $1993$--$95$ and was supplanted by the
energy-drink wave that began with Red Bull's U.S.\ launch in
$1997$ and accelerated through Monster ($2002$). The 1995 cohort
of Class~32 filings was, in retrospect, exactly the cohort that
the next decade's Class~32 industry stopped looking like. The
$\Delta KL$ measure correctly identifies it as a tired cohort.

First, the cannabis/CBD wave of 2014--2020 dominates the top-by-class

Second, the most-and-least innovative industries are surprising.
Tobacco \& Smokers' Articles ($+0.28$ mean $\Delta KL$) and the

industries: Telecommunications, Insurance \& Financial, Building

predicted in advance.

Third, the per-industry survival betas
(Figure~\ref{fig:industry-betas}) show that the
``novelty-is-penalised-at-the-registration-gate'' result reported in
Section~\ref{sec:survival} is \emph{not} universal. In some industries
(notably Common Metals, Hand Tools, Paints \& Coatings) the
coefficient flips sign: novel filings in those industries are
\emph{more} likely to register, not less. A plausible reading is that
in industries where category-creation is rare and meaningful, an
examiner reading a novel goods/services description perceives a real
new use rather than vague language. Software (and pharmaceuticals,
chemicals) follow the opposite pattern: novel filings face slower
examination, more office actions, and lower registration rates,
consistent with examiners pushing back on speculative or boilerplate
broadenings of established categories.

\paragraph{Do industries in flux earn supernumerary returns?}
Across the $44$ NICE classes with adequate data, we computed four
flux measures per class (mean $KL_\text{pros}$, mean $\Delta KL$,
std $\Delta KL$, mean $|\Delta KL|$) and four financial outcomes
(median gross margin, median operating margin, median 4y excess return
vs.\ S\&P 500, median 5y excess return). Excess return is the
firm-mean over firm-years in our matched panel,
$\text{ret}_{4y} - \text{sp500\_ret}_{4y}$, aggregated as a
class-level median to suppress single-firm outliers. We use median
rather than mean because a handful of small public firms in
mechanical-tool industries showed outsized returns that distorted
class means by orders of magnitude.

\textbf{The answer is no.} The cross-industry Pearson correlation
between flux intensity (mean $|\Delta KL|$) and median 4y excess
return is $-0.09$ (Spearman $+0.01$, $n = 42$ industries with
returns data). For 5y excess return: Pearson $-0.05$, Spearman
$+0.02$. The mean prospective KL is similarly uncorrelated with
returns. Industries in flux do not generate excess returns over the
S\&P 500 in this corpus.

The cross-industry pattern that \emph{is} present is on margins, not
on returns: industries with higher flux have systematically
\emph{lower} median gross margins (Pearson $-0.22$, Spearman
$-0.20$, for mean $|\Delta KL|$). High-flux industries are less
profitable per dollar of revenue on average, consistent with such
industries being more competitive (entry-heavy, churn-heavy). The
market appears to price in the lower margins; investors are not
compensated with excess returns for taking on flux risk at the
industry level. Table~\ref{tab:industry-flux-vs-returns} reports
the cross-industry correlation matrix;
Figure~\ref{fig:industry-flux-vs-returns} plots the four key
scatter pairs.

\textbf{Reconciliation with the firm-level result.} The firm-level
U-shape on $\Delta KL$ (Section~\ref{sec:u-shape}) says that
\emph{within} an industry, firms with flux-extreme vocabulary
outperform the flux-neutral middle on survival. The industry-level
null on excess returns is consistent with this: a high-flux
industry contains both flux-extreme winners and flux-neutral losers,
and after the within-industry sorting plays out the industry-mean
return is approximately flat. The flux signal is a within-industry
selection statistic, not an across-industry valuation premium.
``Invest in the high-flux industry'' is not a strategy; ``invest in
the flux-extreme firms within an industry'' might be.

\section{What is the construct, really?}
\label{sec:construct}

We have called $\Delta KL$ an ``innovation'' score throughout, but
the empirical pattern this paper documents is consistent with a
plainer description.

The two-way pattern --- both extremes outperform the middle, both
on survival
(Section~\ref{sec:u-shape}) and on financial variance
(Section~\ref{sec:financials}) --- is consistent with the
construct measuring \emph{how unconventional the firm's bets are},
in either direction. Filings with very high $\Delta KL$ are
unconventional in the direction of category creation; filings with
very low $\Delta KL$ are unconventional in the direction of
templated boilerplate that examiners and competitors leave alone.
Filings with $\Delta KL \approx 0$ are doing what the rest of the
industry was already doing, and unsurprisingly that is what the
data predict for them: average outcomes, average failure rate,
average margins, average returns.

If we step back from the language of ``innovation'' and ask only
what the construct actually correlates with:
\begin{itemize}
  \item It is essentially uncorrelated with patent counts ($r =
        +0.01$ in the matched panel) and with Crunchbase total
        funding ($r = 0.00$); modestly correlated with R\&D-to-revenue
        ratio ($r = +0.13$).
  \item It picks out qualitatively different firms than those
        proxies pick out (Section~\ref{sec:financials}'s patent
        comparison).
  \item It predicts firm-year stock returns above the S\&P~500 on
        a horizon of two-to-five years
        (Section~\ref{sec:returns}); on the firm-debut variant the
        peak is at four years and the magnitude is approximately
        $+28$ percentage points per standard deviation. The peak
        is several years out, not immediate.
  \item It also predicts within-firm gross-margin variance
        (Section~\ref{sec:financials}). Firms whose recent
        $\Delta KL$ is high earn higher mean margins \emph{and}
        spend more years far from those means.
\end{itemize}

These properties are consistent with a simple summary: \textbf{the
construct measures the smartness, and the riskiness, of the bets a
firm makes when it names its products}. Average bets ($\Delta KL$
near zero) earn average outcomes. Bets that depart from convention
in either direction get rewarded or punished above-average; the
rewards take several years to arrive, and the variance of outcomes
is correspondingly larger. Firms with novel bets that the rest of
the industry then imitates --- the high-$\Delta KL$ filings ---
are the ones that capture the rewards in the right tail; firms
with novel bets nobody adopts pay the cost in the left tail.
Boilerplate filings ($\Delta KL$ very negative) are a different
kind of bet: a bet that the existing language is good enough,
that defensibility is more important than category creation. The
data say that bet also works, but on a flatter return distribution.

This is, at the construct level, an evidentiary claim that
\emph{ideas matter}, in a fairly literal sense: a firm whose
trademark filings depart from the industry-mean vocabulary in any
direction has measurably different financial dynamics than one
whose filings hew to the centre. The five-year half-life of the
return effect suggests that good ideas need time to ferment;
imitators have to recognise the new category and copy it before
the originator's claim to it becomes valuable. The sub-additivity
between this measure and patent counts in the $R^2$ decomposition
(Section~\ref{sec:financials}) means we are not just re-discovering
patents in a different guise. Whatever this construct is measuring,
it is measuring something separately predictive of firm
performance.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Two response curves, two theories.}
The empirical pattern we document is best read as two
saturating-exponential response curves meeting at $\Delta KL \approx
0$. On the innovation side ($\Delta KL > 0$) the curve is the
empirical content of optimal distinctiveness (Deephouse 1999;
Zhao et al.\ 2017) translated from \emph{level} of distinctiveness to
\emph{directional} vocabulary flux: survival rises with prospective
novelty, concave-down, with diminishing returns to additional
$\Delta KL$ and no inverted-$U$ inflection at the extreme. What we
do not find is the strict Deephouse prediction that survival turns
down at high distinctiveness; what we do find is the underlying
empirical claim of the optimal-distinctiveness literature ---
distinctiveness pays with diminishing returns --- in an explicit
functional form ($a = 0.995$, $b = -0.55$, $c = 0.063$ on the debut
panel; $R^2 = 0.499$ across $20$ bins). On the harvest side
($\Delta KL < 0$) the same functional form fits much more tightly
($R^2 = 0.978$) and tells a complementary story: survival rises
\emph{faster} with $|\Delta KL|$, saturates closer to the origin,
and reaches an asymptote of $\sim 0.57$. The interpretation is
\emph{increasing returns to late-stage imitation}: the deeper a
filing sits into the receding vocabulary of its category, the more
durable its mark turns out to be. The two extractive strategies
are recognisable in the token data: the negative extreme picks out
clearly sunsetting industries (typesetting in the early 1990s as
desktop publishing arrived, the pre-platform hospitality bundle in
the early 2000s as Expedia disintermediated, e-cigarette
boilerplate of 2014--2015 just before the FDA Deeming Rule); the
positive extreme picks out the legalisation+platform-shift waves
(cannabis, crypto, NFTs, vape, plant-based, EV). \emph{Both
strategies pay}, but they pay through opposite functional forms:
diminishing returns to novelty on one side, increasing returns to
imitation on the other.

\paragraph{Mechanism is not separable.}
Whether the survival lift at negative $\Delta KL$ reflects
intelligent harvesting (firms recognising an industry transition and
extracting from the legacy side), passive late-occupation (firms whose
business naturally uses the receding vocabulary because they are
doing the legacy thing), or selection on an unobservable correlate
(firms that survive long enough to file legacy-vocabulary trademarks
have already proved durable) is not separable in the trademark
record. The unmistakable empirical pattern is that flux-neutral
filings die at the highest rate in the corpus, in $27$ of $44$
industries, at the token level, and \emph{even within the same firm}:
the firm-persistence test
(Section~\ref{sec:u-shape}, falsification \#6) confirms the U
survives within firms that demonstrably abandoned some trademarks
but not all. The signal is filing-level, not firm-level.

\paragraph{The ``arrows in the back'' resolution.}
At the trademark gate, novelty is \emph{rewarded}, not penalised.
The most prospectively surprising filings clear examination at higher
rates and persist longer through the renewal cycle. The mousetrap
pattern dominates the survival outcomes \emph{at the filing level}.
The pioneer-ladder analysis (Section~\ref{sec:pioneer-ladder}) gives
a more nuanced firm-level view: the very-first $10$--$50$ adopters
of category vocabulary do badly (the arrows), the fast-follower band
at ranks $50$--$1{,}000$ does best (the mousetrap-builders), and the
mass late wave is uniformly worst. Lieberman \& Montgomery (1988)
and Suarez \& Lanzolla (2007) anticipated this empirical pattern in
the management literature; the trademark corpus provides large-$n$
evidence at the level of business-description vocabulary.

The arrows-in-the-back signal does, however, show up financially. The
matched panel of $1{,}597$ public firms reveals a clear pioneer-bet
structure: high-novelty firms simultaneously earn higher mean gross
margins (Section~\ref{sec:financials}) \emph{and} display wider
within-firm gross-margin standard deviations (Table~\ref{tab:variance}).
Some pioneers succeed; others do not; the survivors carry the average.
At the trademark registrar's office novelty is a positive signal, but
in the marketplace it raises the variance of outcomes in addition to
the mean.

To make the underlying mechanics concrete,
Table~\ref{tab:tokens} reports a small set of marks together with the
top dictionary tokens that drove their prospective and retrospective
surprise. Filings that score high on prospective surprise share a common
property: they contain tokens (\texttt{generative artificial},
\texttt{seltzer}, \texttt{cannabis}) that are common in the future
corpus but rare in the look-behind window. Filings that score low ---
or even negative --- on $\Delta KL$ contain tokens whose temporal
distribution is the opposite.

\subsection*{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Goods/services text is one of several novelty channels.}
        Brand novelty (\textsc{Liquid Death}), design novelty
        (\textsc{AirPods} casing), and pricing novelty are invisible to
        this corpus. Estimates here should be read as
        innovation-in-business-description novelty.
  \item \textbf{The crosswalk is exact-match only.} A fuzzy-match second
        pass and manual review of the top-by-volume USPTO owners would
        plausibly double or triple the matched panel size and reduce
        survivorship bias toward firms that did not change their
        registered name.
  \item \textbf{Survivorship bias in the financial panel.} Firms appear
        in SEC FSDS only while public. Pre-IPO innovators (Anthropic,
        OpenAI, Stripe at most timepoints) and dead-but-once-public
        firms drop out. The financials reward we estimate is conditional
        on already being a public concern.
  \item \textbf{The reference distributions are smoothed multinomials,
        not topic models.} An LDA replication is the obvious
        artifact-check.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Next steps}
The cross-industry universal sweep (all 45 NICE classes pooled) would
pin down which industries are innovative by this measure and would
allow new-class births (cannabis beverages 2018, generative AI tools
2022) to be scored against a cross-industry background. A
fuzzy-match crosswalk would expand the financial panel from $\sim 1{,}400$
firms toward the ${\sim}5{,}000$ ceiling of the SEC EDGAR universe. With
either or both extensions in place, the natural follow-up is a
\emph{variance} regression: do high-$\Delta KL$ firms exhibit higher
margin variance, the empirical fingerprint of pioneers either
spectacularly succeeding or spectacularly failing?

\section{Falsification battery for the U-shape}
\label{app:falsification}

The compact summary in Section~\ref{sec:u-shape}
(Table~\ref{tab:alternatives}) listed seven candidate explanations
for the survival U-shape on $\Delta KL$ and the outcome of each
re-fit. This appendix narrates each check in full.

\paragraph{(1) Foreign-extension hypothesis is rejected.}
A common worry is that the very-stale (low-$\Delta KL$) end is
inflated by Madrid-Protocol or Section-44 international extensions
of marks already registered abroad --- such marks would use
vocabulary established years before they reached the US filing
record. Using a foreign-owner proxy (name suffixes \texttt{GMBH},
\texttt{S.A.}, \texttt{AB}, \texttt{KK}, \texttt{Pty}, \texttt{Pte},
\texttt{PLC}, \texttt{N.V.}, \texttt{B.V.}, etc.; $3.8\%$ of the
sample) and re-fitting on US-only and foreign-only sub-samples,
the quadratic coefficient is essentially identical ($+0.0078$ and
$+0.0100$ respectively). The U-shape is not a foreign-extension
artefact.

\paragraph{(2) The U is present in both first-time and recurring
filings} ($\beta_{\text{quad}} = +0.0080$ vs.\ $+0.0140$), with
recurring filings showing a slightly stronger curvature. The
fast-followers-do-best pattern from
Section~\ref{sec:pioneer-ladder} is consistent with stronger
curvature in the recurring subset (firms that are still filing
are firms that are still operating).

\paragraph{(3) The financial U-shape is essentially absent.}
A quadratic refit of the firm-year gross-margin OLS gives a linear
coefficient indistinguishable from zero ($\beta_{\text{lin}} =
+0.002$, SE $0.005$) and a marginally negative quadratic
($\beta_{\text{quad}} = -0.002$, SE $0.001$) on $8{,}600$
firm-years. \textbf{The U-shape lives in survival; gross margin
scales roughly linearly with $\Delta KL$.} The
$+3.2$ pp / $\sigma$ linear effect we report in
Section~\ref{sec:financials} is the right summary.

\paragraph{(4) Survivor bias from large incumbents partially supports
the left arm.} Mean firm filing-volume by $\Delta KL$ decile shows
the bottom decile is dominated by larger filers (mean firm-size
$103.7$ filings) than the middle deciles ($63$--$71$); the top
decile is back to $71$. Templated marks disproportionately come
from firms with deep portfolios who can afford to file --- and
maintain --- many similar boilerplate marks. This is consistent
with part of the left-arm uplift being \emph{firm capability}
rather than \emph{filing distinctiveness}.

\paragraph{(5) The ``Liquid Death'' hypothesis is rejected.}
A sceptic might worry that the bottom-decile arm is high-novelty
firms whose products are described in old vocabulary --- like
Liquid Death's canned water marketed as ``still water; sparkling
water.'' Splitting the bottom decile by goods/services text length
does not show this: the shortest quartile and the longest quartile
both renew at $44$--$46\%$ at five years. Templated marks are not
novel products in disguise; they really are templated, and they
really do survive.

\paragraph{(6) The U is filing-level, not firm-level.}
A further worry: the U-shape might be a property of the firms that
file rather than of any given filing --- ``on-trend'' firms might
simply fail more often, dragging their filings down, while
``harvest'' and ``innovate'' firms might survive and keep their
filings alive collectively. To test this we classified each firm
with $\geq 3$ clean filings by whether \emph{all} of its filings
survived 5y (``always-survives,'' $197{,}583$ firms), \emph{all}
failed (``never-survives,'' $253{,}064$ firms), or some survived
while others were let lapse (``mixed,'' $349{,}819$ firms). In the
mixed cohort --- firms that demonstrably persisted past at least
one Section-8 lapse --- the U-shape in filing-level survival by
$\Delta KL$ is preserved: bottom-decile rate $0.551$, middle
$0.479$, top $0.508$ (depth $\approx +0.07$ relative to middle).
Within the same firm, filings whose vocabulary places them at
either flux extreme are more likely to be the ones the firm keeps
alive than the flux-neutral filings the firm lets lapse. The U is
therefore a property of the specific filing, not just of the firm
behind it; trademarks on the flux-neutral ridge really are less
valuable to the firms that file them.
Figure~\ref{fig:firm-persistence} shows the three cohorts.

\paragraph{(7) Imitated-but-failed waves: the blockchain check.}
A natural counterexample to the ``imitated novelty pays'' claim is
to ask whether any recognisable hype wave was both widely imitated
(high $\Delta KL$) and disproportionately a failure. The
blockchain / cryptocurrency / NFT / web3 cohort is a clean test:
$87{,}463$ filings 1995--2025 mention at least one crypto-related
keyword in their goods/services text; $9{,}408$ are clean by our
usual restrictions. The cohort's mean $\Delta KL$ is $+0.412$ vs.\
$-0.002$ for the corpus average --- a genuinely high-novelty wave.
Survival-wise the wave is \emph{out-performing}, not failing:
registration rate $51.5\%$ vs.\ $48.3\%$ corpus mean; five-year
survival $51.4\%$ vs.\ $39.1\%$ corpus mean. Restricted to the top
decile of $\Delta KL$, the crypto cohort beats non-crypto top-decile
filings on both margins ($57.3\%$ vs.\ $47.5\%$ on registration;
$57.2\%$ vs.\ $40.9\%$ on five-year survival). Whatever else can
be said about the blockchain wave's commercial track record, the
trademark filings themselves are surviving well, consistent with
the same likelihood-of-confusion mechanic --- the crypto vocabulary
did not yet exist in the trademark register before the wave, so
applicants did not collide with prior marks.

\paragraph{(8) Temporal stability: the U is present year-by-year.}
The cleanest test of whether the pooled U is an aggregation artefact
is to refit the quadratic separately for each filing-year cohort and
ask whether the U appears in individual years or only when years
are pooled. We fit a logistic regression
$P(\text{survived\_5y}) = \mathrm{logit}^{-1}(\beta_0 + \beta_1
z(\Delta KL) + \beta_2 z(\Delta KL)^2 + \gamma \log(n_\text{terms}))$
separately for each year 1990--2021, both pooled across all 44 classes
and for five representative classes covering the range of cross-
sectional outcomes (Pharmaceuticals 005, Software \& Electronics 009,
Paper \& Print 016, Beer \& Soft Drinks 032, Telecommunications 038).
Figure~\ref{fig:u-shape-temporal} plots the per-year $\beta_2$ with
95\% CIs alongside NBER recession bars; the underlying per-year
counts are in \texttt{u\_shape\_temporal\_stability.csv}.

The pooled per-year coefficient is positive in $24$ of $32$ years and
\emph{significantly} positive ($t > 1.96$) in $23$ of $32$. It is
significantly inverted in only $4$ of $32$ years. The U is present in
the 1990s, in the 2000s, and in the 2010s; it strengthens slightly in
the 2020 cohort but does not depend on a single decade. Pharmaceuticals
is the cleanest case at the class level: $22 / 32$ years
significantly positive, $0$ significantly inverted. Software is
$19 / 32$ significantly positive, $2$ significantly inverted, with the
inverted years concentrated in the late-1990s pre-dot-com cohort.
Telecommunications is genuinely inverted in $10 / 32$ years; the
inverted-U cross-sectional result for that class is not a pooling
artefact but a real industry-level phenomenon.

The U does not track the macro cycle in a clean way. The 2001
dot-com recession is the one year in which the pooled coefficient
softens (it remains positive but only marginally significant); the
2008--09 financial crisis leaves the pooled coefficient roughly
unchanged; the 2020 recession shows a \emph{strengthening} of the
U. Recessions do not produce or destroy the U-shape; the U-shape
is more stable than the macro cycle is.

The temporal-stability test answers the question of whether the U is
a property of specific vintages of firms that happen to cluster at
flux-extreme positions. It is not: the pattern holds in nearly every
cohort independently.

\paragraph{(9) Competition-density mediation.}
The most-revealing falsification. We took $10{,}000$ stratified
Class-9 (Software \& Electronics) focal filings, sampled $1{,}000$
per $\Delta KL$ decile. For each focal filing $F$ at filing-date
$t_F$, we vectorised the entire Class-9 corpus with TF-IDF
(unigrams + bigrams, custom analyzer respecting semicolons,
$\text{min\_df} = 50$, $\text{max\_df} = 0.5$, L2-normalised),
restricted candidate neighbours to filings in $[t_F - 2y,\,
t_F + 5y]$ (the focal's own 5-year survival window plus a 2-year
pre-existing-competition lead-in), computed cosine similarity, and
kept neighbours with sim $\geq 0.7$. For each focal we then counted
\texttt{total\_competitors} (all such neighbours) and
\texttt{live\_competitors} (the subset whose own \texttt{survived\_5y}
was true).

The empirical result is in Table~\ref{tab:competition-density-bins}
and Figure~\ref{fig:competition-density}. By $\Delta KL$ decile, mean
live competitors goes:
$20.1, 20.0, 18.5, 12.9, 14.3, 11.9, 10.8, 12.8, 12.8, 31.4$.
The flux-neutral middle is in clusters with the fewest surviving
peers ($\sim 11$--$14$); both flux extremes are in clusters with $2$
to $3\times$ more surviving peers. The hypothesis we had pencilled in
--- ``flux-neutral firms are over-competed and the extremes are
blue-ocean'' --- is rejected in the direction of the count. The
extremes are not less-competed; they are in clusters where peers can
also survive.

The mediation regression is decisive on the second question
(``does competition density explain the survival U?''). Logit on
\texttt{survived\_5y} with $z(\Delta KL)$, $z(\Delta KL)^2$,
$\log(n_\text{terms})$, year and year$^2$ controls, with and without
$\log(1 + \text{live\_competitors})$ added:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Without the mediator: $\beta_{(\Delta KL)^2} = +0.0110$
        ($z = +1.43$).
  \item With the mediator: $\beta_{(\Delta KL)^2} = +0.0017$
        ($z = +0.24$) --- $84\%$ attenuation.
  \item Mediator coefficient: $+0.193$ ($z = +11.84$) --- strong,
        positive.
  \item Linear $z(\Delta KL)$ goes from $+0.049$ ($z = +2.15$) to
        $+0.062$ ($z = +2.65$) when the mediator is added: the
        linear flux effect is \emph{not} explained by competition
        density.
\end{itemize}

The interpretation is that the survival U on $\Delta KL$ is largely
mediated by the viability of the surrounding vocabulary cluster ---
filings at flux extremes survive better because their cluster
neighbours also survive --- but the linear ``positive flux helps''
effect is a residual signal beyond cluster viability. The U
\emph{shape} is a niche-viability proxy; the linear ``forward flux is
good'' effect is a flux-specific signal.

\paragraph{(10) External-corpus confound test (Google Books).}
A reader worried that $\Delta KL$ might just be tracking
general-corpus English-language usage would predict near-perfect
correlation between per-token trademark-corpus trajectories and
per-token Google Books n-gram trajectories over the same window. We
tested this on $75$ extreme-flux tokens (25 per stratum, drawn from
Table~\ref{tab:symmetric-flux-tokens}). For each token we pulled the
Google Books English n-gram frequency time series 1985--2021 via the
public viewer endpoint, and compared:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{tm\_lift} = mean trademark-corpus frequency in
        2017--2021 divided by mean in 1985--1989 (per-token).
  \item \texttt{google\_lift} = same computation on Google Books.
\end{itemize}
On the 53 tokens with usable data on both sides, the per-token
correlations are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pearson $r(\log \texttt{tm\_lift}, \log \texttt{google\_lift})
        = +0.466$ (Spearman $+0.388$).
  \item Pearson $r(\text{trademark log-slope}, \text{Google log-slope})
        = +0.443$ (Spearman $+0.344$).
\end{itemize}
The trademark-flux signal is materially correlated with general
English-language usage (about $22\%$ of trademark-flux variance per
token is shared with Google Books), but the bulk of variance ($78\%$)
is trademark-corpus-specific. The signal is partly but not wholly a
reflection of broader linguistic drift; the corpus-specific component
is what carries the survival predictive power.

A cleaner version of this test --- adding a Google-Books-derived
token-flux control to the filing-level survival regression --- is a
natural follow-up. We have not done it because (a) the per-token
correlation is moderate enough that we expect modest attenuation,
not nullification, and (b) the cluster-viability mediator in test
(9) is already the dominant mediator. We flag it as the most
informative extension if a reviewer requests further confound work.

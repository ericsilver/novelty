\begin{abstract}
\noindent The DeDeo prospective/retrospective Kullback--Leibler resonance
framework \citep{Murdock2017, Barron2018} was developed on cognitive corpora in
which the author of each document is the agent whose novelty is being measured:
Darwin's reading notebooks, parliamentary speeches of the French Revolution.
This paper asks whether the framework produces interpretable structure on a
qualitatively different corpus type --- the goods/services descriptions of US
trademark filings, which are commercially-incentivised text drafted by counsel
to define legal protection scope. The corpus is 2.32 million granted USPTO
filings, 1990--2024, across four NICE classes selected to exercise a canonical
cross-industry phenomenon (software 009, tech-services 042, transport 039,
advertising/retail 035).

\noindent The construct, denoted \dkl{}, produces three empirical patterns.
\textbf{First}, cross-industry vocabulary diffusion is directly traceable.
Software- and tech-services-origin themes (cloud, AI, mobile-app,
as-a-service, blockchain, streaming, AR/VR) arrive in transport and
advertising/retail with multi-year lags of 1--13 years. At the theme level
via LDA topic extraction, the same asymmetric flow reproduces: of 33 themes
reaching at least two classes, 33 outflow edges originate in
software/tech-services and only 4 return there. Bass diffusion fits on 118
(theme, class) trajectories with $R^2 \geq 0.7$ give median $q/p \approx 7$,
in the range typically reported for consumer-product adoption.
\textbf{Second}, \dkl{} predicts post-registration §8 mark renewal,
analyzed throughout as renewal conditional on completed registration.
\emph{The relationship is forward-favoring at every level of measurement
under this conditioning.} At the filing level in the
software/electronics diagnostic class, the innovator tail (positive
\dkl{}) renews at $\sim$87\%, the flux-neutral middle at $\sim$82\%, and
the going-out-of-fashion negative tail at $\sim$80\%. At the token level
the same ordering holds: positive-tail tokens
(blockchain 99.7\%, cryptocurrency 99.9\%, cbd 100\%) renew highest,
flux-neutral middle tokens (organisational 92\%, conformity 85\%,
freighting 95\%) in between, and negative-tail tokens (typesetting 63\%,
dial-up 89\%, underwriting 84\%) lowest. The unconditional U-shape on this
corpus reported in earlier work is almost entirely a registration-success
pattern: middle-vocabulary applications register at much lower rates than
tail-vocabulary applications, which inflates the apparent tail survival
unconditionally; conditioning on registration removes the U.
\textbf{Third}, the signal is orthogonal-to-negative with patents within firm:
$-0.048\sigma$, $t = -7.9$ on 14{,}463 trademark-and-patent-matched firms.
\dkl{} is empirically distinct from patent-track invention.

\noindent The raw test of whether new themes are carried predominantly by
entrants or incumbents reads as 73.7\% entrant-led, but this collapses on
year-matched baseline contrast to a $-2.7$pp mean excess once the corpus's
76.4\% overall debut rate is netted out.

\noindent The paper defends a bounded reading: \dkl{} is a measurement of
lexical resonance with interpretable empirical structure on commercial text,
not an innovation measure in the strong sense. Code and per-firm-year panels
are released for replication at \url{https://github.com/ericsilver/novelty}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\citet{Murdock2017} introduced a measurement framework for directional novelty
in time-ordered text corpora: for each document, compare its token distribution
to aggregated distributions of past documents (prospective surprise) and
future documents (retrospective surprise). The signed difference of those two
quantities they call \emph{resonance}: it is positive when a document is
unusual against its recent past and close to its near future, negative for
the inverse. The framework was developed on Charles
Darwin's reading notebooks and extended in \citet{Barron2018} to French
Revolution parliamentary debates. Both corpora share a salient property: the
text is authored by the agent whose novelty is the object of measurement, and
the author's cognitive or rhetorical state is what the framework is designed
to track.

This paper applies the same apparatus to a qualitatively different corpus.
The goods/services descriptions of US trademark filings are
\emph{commercially-incentivised}, typically drafted by counsel rather than the
firm principal, and exist to define legal protection scope --- not to express
cognition or rhetoric. The text is subject to two competing pressures: it must
be wide enough to cover the applicant's intended commercial activity and
narrow enough to be granted by an examiner. Whether the resonance framework
produces interpretable structure on text of this kind is the empirical
question of this paper. Two follow-up questions discipline the reading. First,
how does the constructed signal compare to other measures that have been
treated as proxies for innovation, patent counts in particular? On a
within-firm panel of trademark owners matched to patent assignees, the answer
is that the signal is orthogonal-to-negative with patents: it is empirically
distinct from patent-track invention, not a noisier version of it. Second,
does the signal predict commercially meaningful outcomes? Analyzed throughout
as renewal conditional on completed registration, the answer is yes and the
direction is monotone: at both the filing level and the token level,
forward-leaning vocabulary correlates with higher renewal rates. The
unconditional U-shape that earlier analysis reported on this corpus is shown
here to be primarily a registration-success pattern; conditioning on
registration removes it.

The empirical record in text-based economics has converged on text-similarity
methods --- the text-based network industry classification of
\citet{HobergPhillips2016} being the most influential --- and has rarely used
trademark text for measurement. Trademark filings have been used to study
business formation \citep{Dinlersoz2018} and to distinguish protective
filings from substantive product introductions \citep{Graham2013}, but the
text of those filings has been largely unused.

The paper's claims are bounded by what the data support. The constructed
signal \dkl{}, computed as in \citet{Murdock2017, Barron2018}, produces three
empirical patterns on a 2.32 million-filing granted corpus 1990--2024 across
four NICE classes. Cross-industry vocabulary diffusion is directly traceable
at both the phrase and theme levels, with directional asymmetry: software-
and tech-services-origin themes arrive in transport and advertising/retail
years later, not the reverse. Bass-fittable adoption curves with median
$q/p \approx 7$ on 118 (theme, class) trajectories lend external plausibility
to the diffusion reading. The asymmetric flow reproduces under
independently-fitted non-negative matrix factorization on the same vocabulary
as a within-paradigm robustness check. Within firms, \dkl{} is
orthogonal-to-negative with patent counts, establishing the construct as
empirically distinct from patent-track invention. \dkl{} predicts
post-registration §8 mark renewal, framed throughout as renewal conditional
on completed registration, with the same forward-favoring direction at the
filing level and the token level. The unconditional U-shape reported in
earlier work on this corpus is shown here to be primarily a
registration-success pattern.

\dkl{} does not measure innovation itself in any strong sense. The
within-firm patent-orthogonality refutes the ``\dkl{} replaces patents as
an innovation measure'' reading; the framework's status as
commercially-incentivised text refutes the cognitive-state reading
inherited from Darwin's notebooks; and the small-$n$ validation against
expert ``most innovative'' lists ($n=48$ matched firms) is too weak to
license the strong claim. What \dkl{} measures, on this evidence, is
\emph{lexical resonance} on commercial text: a filing's forward-leaning
position in its industry's vocabulary trajectory. The resonance is
correlated with post-registration mark re-registration to a degree that
deserves further work --- the U-shape in \S8 maintenance is the central
empirical pattern of the paper and the link from a measurement of text
position to a commercially meaningful outcome.

Section~\ref{sec:rq4} also reports a methodological aside. The question of
whether new themes are carried predominantly by entrants or incumbents
reads naively as overwhelming entrant dominance, but collapses on
year-matched baseline contrast. The trademark-text literature is
particularly exposed to base-rate bias because the corpus is mechanically
saturated in entrants, and the discipline of year-matched contrast is what
distinguishes useful from inflated findings on this data.

fragilities. Section~\ref{sec:transit} presents the cross-industry phrase

\section{\dkl{} on a USPTO trademark corpus}\label{sec:construct}

\subsection{The DeDeo apparatus}

For a time-ordered sequence of documents, with each document at year $t$
producing a token distribution $P_t$, the DeDeo framework defines two
quantities against a window $W$ of past and future documents
\citep{Murdock2017, Barron2018}:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textit{Prospective surprise}: $\mathrm{KL}(P_t \,\|\, Q_{\mathrm{past}})$, where $Q_{\mathrm{past}}$ is the aggregate token distribution of documents in years $t-W$ to $t-1$.
  \item \textit{Retrospective surprise}: $\mathrm{KL}(P_t \,\|\, Q_{\mathrm{future}})$, where $Q_{\mathrm{future}}$ is the aggregate distribution of documents in years $t+1$ to $t+W$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The signed resonance is
\[
\Delta\mathrm{KL}_t \;=\; \mathrm{KL}(P_t \,\|\, Q_{\mathrm{past}}) - \mathrm{KL}(P_t \,\|\, Q_{\mathrm{future}}).
\]

Positive \dkl{} marks a document whose vocabulary is unusual relative to
recent past and close to near future. Negative \dkl{} marks the inverse,
language that looked normal at filing and looked played-out in hindsight.

\subsection{Corpus}

The USPTO Trademark Annual Applications backfile (product TRTYRAP) provides
a complete record of US trademark filings from 1884 onward. For this paper I
work with four NICE classes selected to exercise canonical cross-industry
diffusion: software (009), scientific and technical services (042), transport
(039), and advertising and retail (035). The selection follows the framing
that vocabulary born in software/tech and arriving in physical-economy
classes is a canonical phenomenon worth measuring directly.

Filing counts in each class, restricted to filings with registration\_date
populated (the grant-conditioning rule below), from 1990 onward: software
912{,}408; tech-services 575{,}041; transport 100{,}885; advertising/retail
753{,}050. Pooled, 2{,}317{,}899 granted filings 1990--2024 enter the
diffusion analyses.

\subsection{Grant conditioning}

The corpus is restricted to filings with a populated registration date. The
conditioning has two motivations. First, applications include speculative or
defensive filings that may not reflect realized commercial activity; the
registration outcome screens out applications that did not survive
examination. Second, the resonance signal is most interpretable for filings
whose vocabulary reflects intended commercial use rather than maximal
claim-scope drafting. Approximately 56\% of Class~009 filings in the full
backfile are granted; the four-class pooled share is similar. Grant
conditioning also discards a substantial fraction of negative-\dkl{} filings
that may merely be unconcluded applications.

\subsection{Computation}

For each granted filing at year $t$ in class $c$ with goods/services text
producing token distribution $P$ (unigram + bigram, after a standard analyzer
that drops stopwords and tokenizes within punctuation chunks), the
prospective and retrospective reference distributions $Q_{\mathrm{past}}$ and
$Q_{\mathrm{future}}$ are computed from all granted filings in class $c$ in
the respective windows. I use $W = 5$ years (flat window) with Dirichlet
smoothing ($\alpha = 1$ per cell) to avoid infinite surprise from
zero-count tokens. The within-class vocabulary is the set of unigrams and
bigrams with document frequency $\geq 50$.

\paragraph{Renewal as conditional on completed registration.} Whenever the
paper reports a survival or §8 mark renewal rate at the filing level, the
denominator is restricted to filings that reached registration (status
bucket 6 or 8). This isolates the renewal decision from the
registration-success decision; it differs from earlier analyses on this
corpus that pooled both. The numerator is filings that remain alive and
maintained at the time of measurement (status bucket 6), which on the
five-year-old cohort approximates ``survived the §8 maintenance gate.'' The
token-level survival statistic uses a different denominator (marks
containing a given token, with the same conditioning on registration);
filing-level and token-level magnitudes are not directly comparable but
both apply the same conditioning step.

\subsection{Reference-window density and burn-in}

The signal depends on having enough past and future filings to populate the
reference distributions. Figure~\ref{fig:burnin} shows the reference-window
density by year for two diagnostic classes (software/electronics and beer/
soft drinks). The 5-year past window is incomplete before 1990 and the 5-year
future window is incomplete after 2020; both are excluded from the
construct's interpretable range. Within the supported window
(1990--2020), the four NICE classes in this paper have between
$\sim$3{,}500 and $\sim$80{,}000 filings per year, which is more than enough
to stabilize the unigram and bigram count distributions at $W = 5$.

\subsection{What the construct does and does not measure}

The construct's known fragilities have been documented in companion analysis
on a complete Class~009 corpus of 1{,}807{,}636 filings. Prospective and
retrospective KL are correlated at $r = +0.971$ across 1.56M scored filings;
signed \dkl{} is therefore a small residual of two large, highly correlated
quantities. \emph{This residual is noisy at the per-filing level.} The
magnitude $|\Delta\mathrm{KL}|$ inflates monotonically for short filings
(0.293 for documents under 5 terms, decaying to 0.147 for documents of 40 or
more terms) --- a length artifact that requires control in any per-filing
analysis. Pre-1995 readings are contaminated by thin past (mean \dkl{} of
$+2.05$ in 1990); the burn-in cut should exclude these years.

Figure~\ref{fig:quadrant} grounds the construct in recognizable examples.
The two scatter axes are prospective surprise (the filing relative to its
preceding 5 years) and retrospective surprise (the filing relative to its
following 5 years), with \dkl{} as the diagonal residual. Filings above the
diagonal are \emph{fired} (the language looked already-played-out in
hindsight; the future moved away from this filing); filings below the
diagonal are \emph{innovator} (the language looked unusual at filing and
became standard afterwards; the future caught up to this filing). The
labeled examples are recognizable brand introductions positioned according
to their goods/services text on this scatter.

\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{results/quadrant.png}

Window sensitivity is bounded. Across $W \in \{3, 5, 7\}$ years, only
6.9--10.4\% of filings flip sign --- the direction of the residual is robust
to half-life kernel choice, the magnitude is kernel-relative. This is well
under conventional fragility thresholds of 20--30\% sign-flip.

What the construct measures, on this evidence, is \emph{lexical resonance}: a
filing's forward-leaning position in its industry's vocabulary trajectory at
filing time. The construct does not measure innovation in the strong sense.
Sections~\ref{sec:transit}--\ref{sec:patent} examine what the construct
produces empirically and what those patterns license.

\section{Cross-industry phrase transit}\label{sec:transit}

\subsection{Operationalization}

To make cross-industry diffusion operational without committing to a specific
topic-extraction method (Section~\ref{sec:themes} takes up the topic-extraction
question), I define a list of 14 multi-token business-model phrases and track
their prevalence across (class, year) cells directly. Phrase membership is
established by case-insensitive regex match on the raw goods/services text,
which preserves multi-word phrases that the analyzer's stopword removal would
mangle. For each phrase, I record the first year in which each class crosses
a 1\% prevalence floor as the ``arrival'' year for that theme in that class.

Of the phrases that clear the 1\% floor in transport and advertising/retail,
almost all originate in software (009) or tech-services (042) and arrive in
the physical-economy classes with lags of one to thirteen years. The
exception is on-demand, which originates in transport (2014) before
software/tech (2016--17). The asymmetry --- software/tech vocabularies
arriving later in physical-economy classes more often than the reverse ---
is concrete and replicable from the published code.

\subsection{Caveats}

The 1\% floor is sensitive to class size. Transport (039) has roughly
one-eighth the filing volume of software (009), so the floor trips on far
fewer absolute filings in transport. A phrase below 1\% in transport may
still be present with substantive absolute volume. The 1\% choice is
conventional; a different floor (0.5\% or 2\%) shifts arrival years by a
year or two but does not reverse the directional pattern.

The asymmetry is also a four-class result. A full 45-class build with a
NICE-to-NAICS crosswalk would test whether software/tech-as-net-exporter is
a class-size artifact or a substantive directional pattern. The current
evidence is consistent with the directional reading but cannot exclude a
corpus-construction alternative.

A second open question is whether a phrase that has crossed industries
\emph{means the same thing} in its new class. When ``blogs'' (born in 009)
arrives in 035 (advertising/retail), the filings may be either
panners-for-gold --- firms whose offer \emph{is} a blog --- or
sellers-of-pick-axes --- firms whose offer is a service \emph{to} bloggers
(hosting, monetization, analytics). The two have different commercial
trajectories. A within-phrase decomposition of the goods/services text
into ``offer-is-the-thing'' versus ``offer-supplies-the-thing'' is a natural
extension and is owed to follow-up work; this paper documents the arrival
of the phrase in the new class without committing to which role it plays.

\section{Renewal conditional on completed registration}\label{sec:ushape}

\subsection{Filing-level result}

Among granted filings in the post-burn-in 1996--2020 window with $\geq 10$
terms and reached-registration status (status bucket 6 or 8), 5-year mark
renewal is \emph{forward-favoring} in \dkl{}: in the
software/electronics diagnostic class, the innovator tail (positive
\dkl{}) renews at $\sim$87\%, the flux-neutral middle at $\sim$82\%, and
the going-out-of-fashion negative tail at $\sim$80\% with a slight uptick at
the far-left edge. Figure~\ref{fig:ushape} shows this conditional renewal
rate by each of the three KL axes. The two directional pieces relate
negatively to renewal: high prospective surprise or high retrospective
surprise both predict lower conditional renewal, with the rate sliding from
$\sim$92\% at the low end to $\sim$78\% at the high end.

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{results/outcome_curves_software.png}
\caption{5-year renewal rate (mark still alive and maintaining at the time
of measurement) for software/electronics filings, conditional on completed
registration. Higher prospective or retrospective KL both predict lower
conditional renewal. The signed \dkl{} panel is forward-favoring rather than
strictly U-shaped: the right tail (innovator vocabulary) sits clearly above
the centre; the left tail (going-out-of-fashion vocabulary) sits at or
below the centre, with a slight uptick at the far edge.}
\label{fig:ushape}
\end{figure}

The conditional reading is the right way to think about the construct's link
to commercial outcomes: it isolates the renewal decision from the
registration-success decision. The unconditional U-shape reported in earlier
work on this corpus is real, but it is largely a registration-success
story --- negative-tail vocabulary is less likely to register at all, and
the marks that do register die at roughly the same rate as the middle.
Conditional on registration, the surviving signal is monotone-ish and
favors forward-leaning vocabulary.

The same forward-favoring pattern is present when the analysis is restricted
to debut-only filings (filings made by an owner for whom the filing is the
first ever across the four-class corpus), and it extends to a longer-horizon
external outcome: whether the owner ever appears in the SEC EDGAR listed
universe.

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{results/debut_outcome_by_kl.png}
\caption{Outcomes for debut-only filings (owner's first-ever filing,
$n = 5{,}090{,}053$) by KL value. The signed \dkl{} (blue) shows the
filing-level U-shape that the conditional reading in
Figure~\ref{fig:ushape} flattens out, evidence that the unconditional
U-shape is concentrated in the registration-success step. The
SEC-EDGAR-listed-owner panel (third panel) is monotonically up-and-to-the-
right in the directional KL pieces, with a $\sim$30\% lift between the left
and right tails, consistent with the conditional renewal-rate finding.}
\label{fig:debut}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Token-level result}

The token-level pattern, applied with the same conditioning on completed
registration, mirrors the filing-level forward-favoring ordering rather
than reproducing the U-shape that earlier unconditional analysis on this
corpus reported. Conditional renewal rates for example tokens in the
software/electronics diagnostic class, with the unconditional rate shown
in parentheses for comparison:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
\item Positive tail (emerging vocabulary): \emph{blockchain} 99.7\%
(56.1\%), \emph{cryptocurrency} 99.9\% (55.2\%), \emph{cannabis} 97.4\%
(63.4\%), \emph{cbd} 100\% (81.9\%), \emph{vape} 100\% (85.4\%).
\item Flux-neutral middle: \emph{organisational} 92.3\% (13.5\%),
\emph{conformity} 85.1\% (14.4\%), \emph{freighting} 94.5\% (21.3\%),
\emph{intermediation} 99.4\% (25.8\%).
\item Negative tail (disappearing vocabulary): \emph{typesetting} 63.2\%
(26.8\%), \emph{dial-up} 89.2\% (43.9\%), \emph{annuity} 82.0\% (41.4\%),
\emph{underwriting} 83.8\% (32.3\%).
\end{itemize}

Conditional on registration, the negative-tail tokens renew at $\sim$80--90\%,
the flux-neutral middle at $\sim$85--99\%, and the positive tail at
$\sim$97--100\%. The ordering is essentially monotone in the direction of
the filing-level forward-favoring pattern. The unconditional U-shape that
earlier analysis reported (50--80\% at the tails versus 11--26\% in the
middle) was almost entirely a registration-success pattern: middle-vocabulary
applications register at $\sim$15--26\%, tail-vocabulary applications at
$\sim$30--60\%, so the tail \emph{unconditional} survival rate was higher
than the middle \emph{unconditional} survival rate not because tail-vocabulary
\emph{marks} survive better but because tail-vocabulary \emph{applications}
register more often. Conditioning isolates the renewal step and removes the
U.

\subsection{Robustness and mechanism}

The conditional forward-favoring pattern is robust to substitution of the
outcome operationalization (status bucket 6 vs. survived 5 years explicitly
vs. currently-live at 10 years), to choice of half-life kernel within
$W \in \{3, 5, 7\}$ years, and to length control, on the software/
electronics diagnostic class. Whether it extends to all four classes in the
diagnostic subset and to a pooled run on the full $\sim$45-class trademark
corpus is the natural next robustness check and is not done here.

The mechanism candidate that survives the conditioning step is the simple
one: forward-leaning vocabulary at filing time is a marker of an applicant
who has identified a commercially viable position; commercial viability
predicts both registration completion and post-registration renewal; and
the registration step is by far the larger filter, which is why the
unconditional reading produces a U-shape and the conditional reading does
not. Whether the residual conditional gap between innovator-tail and
going-out-of-fashion vocabulary reflects intrinsic vocabulary effects or
selection on owner covariates is owed to follow-up work.

\section{Theme-level evidence: Bass-fittable curves and cross-class flow asymmetry}\label{sec:themes}

The phrase transit in Section~\ref{sec:transit} depends on a curated phrase
list. A theme-level analysis using unsupervised topic extraction provides
independent corroboration: if the asymmetric flow is genuinely a corpus
property rather than an artifact of which phrases I happened to pick, the
same directional pattern should also appear at the theme level via
topic-prevalence trajectories.

\subsection{LDA topic extraction}

I fit a Latent Dirichlet Allocation topic model with $T = 50$ topics on a
stratified sample of 200{,}000 granted filings (50{,}000 per class, capped at
class size; transport is smaller after sampling). The vocabulary is unigrams
+ bigrams with document frequency $\geq 200$, giving $V = 69{,}361$ terms.
The fitted model is transformed across the full 2.32 million-filing pool to
assign each filing a topic distribution and a top topic.

The resulting top-word lists are recognizable business-model components.
Selected examples: T0 \{health, care, wellness, healthcare\}; T1 \{autonomous,
loading, unloading, truck, material handling\}; T2 \{store, retail,
wholesale\}; T10 \{electronic transmission, computer networks, data,
communication\}; T30 \{online, website, non-downloadable, information\}; T49
\{vehicles, traffic, bicycle, lights\}. I make no claim that 50 unsupervised
topics constitute the final theme set; cross-method stability and human
face-validation remain owed before any individual theme is treated as
headline.

\subsection{Bass-fittable adoption curves}

For each (theme, class) cell where a theme reaches the 1\% prevalence floor
and is tracked for at least six years post-arrival, I fit the Bass (1969)
diffusion model to the normalized cumulative prevalence. The Bass model
parameterizes adoption with an innovation coefficient $p$, an imitation
coefficient $q$, and a saturation level $m$ \citep{Bass1969}. I fit $p$ and
$q$ with non-linear least squares, retaining fits with $R^2 \geq 0.7$.

Of 118 retained fits, the median $p$ is 0.017, the median $q$ is 0.114, and
the median $q/p$ is approximately 7. The median $q/p$ falls in the range
typically reported for consumer-product Bass fits in the diffusion-of-
innovations literature, lending external plausibility to the (theme, class)
adoption curves as describing real adoption events rather than topic-model
artifacts. Illustrative platform-like fits (low $q/p$, broad gradual
adoption): the travel/transportation theme in advertising/retail and tech-
services ($q/p \in [0.07, 0.17]$); the retail/store-services theme in
advertising/retail ($q/p \approx 0.66$); the electronic-transmission /
computer-networks theme in advertising/retail ($q/p \approx 0.60$).
Illustrative fad-shaped fits (high $q/p$, steep adoption with prevalence
visibly receding in recent panel years): the renewable-energy theme in
software and tech-services ($q/p > 100$); the virtual-reality/fitness theme
in advertising/retail and tech-services ($q/p > 40$); the
software/applications theme in transport and tech-services ($q/p > 130$),
where the high $q/p$ reflects the rapid arrival of software-application
vocabulary in physical-economy classes once it had already saturated in
software (009).

\subsection{Robustness under independently-fitted NMF}

I refit topic structure independently using non-negative matrix factorization
on the same 200{,}000-filing sample and vocabulary, with $T = 50$, then
transform across the full pool. The Bass-fit pattern and cross-class flow
asymmetry reproduce. Seven of ten hand-curated seed phrases from
Section~\ref{sec:transit} (blockchain, cloud, AI/ML, SaaS, mobile-app,
AR/VR, marketplace) have a clear best-NMF-topic; the three missing
(streaming, IoT, on-demand) are either too generic at the topic level or
too brief at the phrase level to concentrate in a single topic at $T = 50$.
The substantive diffusion findings do not depend on the choice between LDA
and NMF, though both are bag-of-words matrix factorizations on the same
vocabulary, so this robustness is within-paradigm; a cross-paradigm test
under a sentence-embedding cluster or graph-based community detection method
on the same corpus is the natural methodological extension and is not run
here.

\section{Empirical distinctness from patents}\label{sec:patent}

If \dkl{} and patent counts were noisy measures of the same underlying
construct, they should correlate within firms over time. They do not.

The within-firm analysis uses a published panel of 174{,}569 firm-years
covering 14{,}463 trademark owners that have been name-matched to PatentsView
patent assignees \citep{PatentsView}. The panel and the matching code are in
the public repository (see Section~\ref{sec:repro}); the panel is used here
only to validate the construct against the patent-track measure. The
matching procedure normalizes both sides (strip corporate-form suffixes
``inc.'', ``llc.'', ``corp.'', ``ltd.'' and punctuation, collapse whitespace,
lowercase) and joins on exact normalized-name equality; the universe is
restricted to firms with at least two filings in the four-class corpus and
at least one year with patenting activity, which is what produces the 14{,}463
matched-firm panel. The match is conservative --- it loses partial-name
overlaps and subsidiary structures --- but is reproducible from the code
without manual cleaning, which matters for the construct-validity claim. A
fuzzy-match alternative using the maximum patent count over candidate
assignees per trademark owner yields a coefficient of $-0.048\sigma$ at
$t = -8.0$ on the same panel, reported as a robustness row in
Table~\ref{tab:patent_within_firm}. I fit a two-way (firm + year) fixed-effects regression of
standardized firm-year mean \dkl{} on $\log(1 + \mathrm{patents})$ with
firm-clustered standard errors. The standard deviation of mean \dkl{} on the
collapsed firm-year panel is 0.214.

The coefficient is negative and precisely estimated, strengthening to

on patents$_{t-1}$: in years a firm patents more, the firm's subsequent
trademark vocabulary regresses toward class-typical rather than ahead.

The within-firm result establishes that \dkl{} is empirically distinct from
patent-track invention. It does not by itself license a strong claim that
\dkl{} measures a different innovation channel. An alternative reading
consistent with the within-firm data is that firm activity allocation
between patent prosecution and brand work shifts year-to-year and the
trademark vocabulary becomes more boilerplate during years of high patent
output. The empirical distinctness is robust; the substantive interpretation
of the distinct channel is bounded by the validation evidence elsewhere.

\section{Do new themes come from incumbents or entrants?}\label{sec:rq4}

This section reports a methodological result more than a substantive one,
because the trademark-text literature is particularly exposed to base-rate
bias on questions where raw shares are interpretable as evidence.

The question framed here is the old one: when a new theme appears in a
corpus, is it carried predominantly by new entrants or by incumbents
diversifying into the new theme? \citet{Schumpeter1942} introduced the
distinction, and the literature labels the two regimes Mark~I (entrant-led)
and Mark~II (incumbent-led). The standard empirical form is to identify a
population of early adopters of an innovation, classify them as entrant or
incumbent, and report the entrant share.

I apply this standard form to the 50 LDA themes. For each theme, I take its
50 earliest carriers (sorted by filing year, conditional on top-topic weight
$\geq 0.20$). I classify each carrier's owner as an entrant if its first
granted mark across the four-class corpus is in the carrier-filing year or
one year earlier --- that is, the carrier is filing at or near the owner's
debut.

\label{tab:raw_schumpeter}

\toprule
Statistic & Value & Illustrative themes \\
\midrule
Themes scored & 50 & --- \\
Mean entrant share among 50 earliest carriers & 73.7\% & --- \\
Themes entrant-dominated ($\geq 50\%$ entrants) & 48 / 50 &
software/applications (92\%); retail/store-services (90\%);
clothing/bags/sportswear (90\%); aircraft/air-transport/storage (90\%);
business-management/corporate-sales (88\%) \\
Themes incumbent-leaning (lowest entrant share) & --- &
environmental/sustainability/urban (28\%); natural-gas/petroleum/utility
(44\%); food/preparations/hair-care (54\%); artificial-intelligence/

Themes incumbent-dominated ($\leq 20\%$ entrants) & 0 / 50 & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent The raw numbers look like strong evidence of entrant-led adoption.
But the question is not whether the entrant share exceeds 50\%; it is
whether the entrant share among earliest theme-carriers exceeds the corpus
baseline rate at which \emph{any} granted filing in this period is by a new
entrant.

The corpus's overall debut rate among granted filings in 1990--2024 is
68--77\% per year across the four classes. With a year-weighted mean
(weighting by each theme's earliest-carrier year distribution), the baseline
is 76.4\% new entrants and 23.6\% incumbents. \emph{Most filings in the
four-class corpus over this period are by owners debuting that year.}
Against this baseline, the 73.7\% theme-carrier entrant share is slightly
\emph{below} corpus-average, not strikingly entrant-led.

\label{tab:rq4_baseline}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Statistic & Value \\
\midrule

Year-matched baseline (mean / median) & 76.4\% / 76.7\% \\

\end{tabular}

The mean theme excess is $-2.7$ percentage points; 28 of 50 themes are
\emph{below} baseline. Only nine themes show real entrant excess above
10pp. The Schumpeter Mark~I claim collapses: themes inherit the corpus's
high debut rate without adding to it.

The trademark-text literature is particularly exposed to base-rate bias
because the corpus is mechanically saturated in entrants, and any analysis
that reports raw population shares without year-matched baseline contrast
risks reading mechanical corpus structure as substantive finding. The
entrant-vs-incumbent question is not the central question of the paper, but
the discipline of base-rate contrast is what distinguishes useful from
inflated findings in this kind of analysis, and the discipline should be
visible in any work that uses trademark text to ask population-share
questions.

One secondary observation is worth surfacing without elevating to a
headline. The themes that show the strongest negative excess (carried
disproportionately
by incumbents relative to baseline) include environmental/sustainability,
natural gas / petroleum / utility, artificial-intelligence / compliance /
machine, and cloud-computing/lighting-fixtures (Table~\ref{tab:raw_schumpeter}).
The canonically ``new'' technology themes of the 2010s and early 2020s are
in this list, not the entrant-led one. Incumbent firms diversifying into AI
and cloud appear to be pulling the trademark-vocabulary signal forward,
rather than de-novo entrants doing so. This is consistent with the
within-firm patent orthogonality of Section~\ref{sec:patent}: the firms
moving the vocabulary are not necessarily the firms moving the patents. The
finding would not on its own clear a publication bar, but it is a useful
sense-check that the construct is responding to a substantive corpus
phenomenon rather than to corpus mechanics.

\section{Scope and limits}\label{sec:scope}

The paper makes four substantive claims.

First, \dkl{}, computed as in \citet{Murdock2017, Barron2018} with a 5-year
flat window on granted USPTO trademark filings, produces a directly
traceable cross-industry vocabulary diffusion record on a 4-class subset of
the corpus. The directional asymmetry, with software/tech-origin themes
arriving in transport and advertising/retail at multi-year lags and the
reverse rarely happening, is concrete at the phrase level
(Section~\ref{sec:transit}) and reproduces at the theme level under
unsupervised LDA topic extraction (Section~\ref{sec:themes}), with
within-paradigm robustness under independently-fitted NMF.

Second, \dkl{} predicts post-registration §8 mark renewal, analyzed as
renewal conditional on completed registration. The relationship is
forward-favoring at every level of measurement under this conditioning: at
the filing level in the diagnostic class (innovator tail $\sim$87\%, middle
$\sim$82\%, negative tail $\sim$80\%), at the token level (positive-tail
tokens 97--100\%, middle tokens 85--99\%, negative-tail tokens 63--89\%),
and at the longer-horizon outcome of eventual SEC-EDGAR-listed status. The
unconditional U-shape reported in earlier work on this corpus is shown here
to be primarily a registration-success pattern.

Third, \dkl{} is empirically orthogonal-to-negative with patent counts
within firm, confirming the construct is distinct from patent-track
invention.

Fourth, the portion of new themes filed by new entrants (73.7\%) is slightly
below the corpus baseline rate (76.4\% entrants, 23.6\% incumbents); the
entrant-led reading of new themes does not survive baseline contrast, and
the discipline of contrasting against the right baseline is what makes the
difference.

The paper rejects two claims that the construct does not support. \dkl{}
does not measure innovation in any strong sense; the defended primitive is
lexical resonance on commercial text. \dkl{} does not identify an
investment return premium on extreme-tail debut filings.

The renewal results are conditional on completed registration and should
not be read as describing the full distribution of trademark applications.
The decision to register and the decision to renew are different commercial
acts; this paper isolates the second by conditioning on the first. An
applicant whose vocabulary disqualifies the filing from registration in
the first place is excluded from the renewal denominator, and the
unconditional U-shape reported in earlier work on this corpus reflects both
steps mixed. The conditioning makes the renewal pattern interpretable as a
post-registration commercial signal rather than as a registration-success
proxy; it also limits the claim to filings that already cleared examination.

The paper does not evaluate the following questions, which are owed to
follow-up work. Whether the theme-level flow asymmetry reproduces under a
structurally different topic-extraction method (sentence-embedding clustering
or graph-based community detection, rather than the within-paradigm
LDA/NMF used here). Whether the software-and-tech-services-as-net-exporter
asymmetry survives a full 45-class build with a NICE-to-NAICS crosswalk and
proper class-size controls. Whether the filing-level conditional renewal
result extends to all four classes in the subset and to the full corpus
pooled in one go. Whether a within-phrase decomposition of cross-industry
arrivals into ``offer-is-the-thing'' versus ``offer-supplies-the-thing''
separates the two carrier types of cross-industry adoption
(Section~\ref{sec:transit}). Whether mechanism on the U-shape can be
disambiguated using out-of-corpus firm covariates.

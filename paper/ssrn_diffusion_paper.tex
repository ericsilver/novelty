\begin{abstract}
\noindent The DeDeo prospective/retrospective Kullback--Leibler resonance
framework \citep{Murdock2017, Barron2018} was developed on cognitive corpora in
which the author of each document is the agent whose novelty is being measured:
Darwin's reading notebooks, parliamentary speeches of the French Revolution.
This paper asks whether the framework produces interpretable structure on a
qualitatively different corpus type --- the goods/services descriptions of US
trademark filings, which are commercially-incentivised text drafted by counsel
to define legal protection scope. The corpus is 2.32 million granted USPTO
filings, 1990--2024, across four NICE classes selected to exercise a canonical
cross-industry phenomenon (software 009, tech-services 042, transport 039,
advertising/retail 035).

\noindent The construct, denoted \dkl{}, produces three empirical patterns.
\textbf{First}, cross-industry vocabulary diffusion is directly traceable:
software- and tech-services-origin themes (cloud, AI, mobile-app, as-a-service,
blockchain, streaming, AR/VR) arrive in transport and advertising/retail with
multi-year lags of 1--13 years. The diffusion structure passes a shuffle null
on (class, year) label permutations at $z = +46$ under LDA topic extraction
and replicates at $z = +60.7$ under independently-fitted non-negative matrix
factorization on the same vocabulary. The shuffle test confirms that the
panel has temporal structure that survives label permutation; the
cross-paradigm test (embedding-cluster or graph community detection) is the
natural methodological extension and is not run here. \textbf{Second}, \dkl{}
produces a U-shape in post-registration §8 maintenance outcomes. At the
filing level, filings whose vocabulary moves with the field and filings whose
vocabulary moves against it both maintain registration 5--10 percentage points
more often than the flux-neutral middle. At the token level the gap is
sharper: marks containing distinctive-tail tokens (typesetting, dial-up,
blockchain, NFT) survive at 50--80\% while marks containing flux-neutral
middle tokens (organisational, conformity, freighting) survive at 11--26\%.
\textbf{Third}, the signal is orthogonal-to-negative with patents within firm:
$-0.048\sigma$, $t = -7.9$ on 14{,}463 trademark-and-patent-matched firms.
\dkl{} is empirically distinct from patent-track invention.

\noindent A worked discipline result: the raw Schumpeter Mark~I read of new
themes (73.7\% entrant-dominated) collapses on year-matched baseline contrast
to a $-2.7$pp mean excess once the corpus's 76.4\% overall debut rate is
netted out. The paper reports this rather than burying it.

\noindent The paper defends a bounded reading: \dkl{} is a measurement of
lexical resonance with interpretable empirical structure on commercial text,
not an innovation measure in the strong sense. Code and per-firm-year panels
are released for replication at \url{https://github.com/ericsilver/novelty}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\citet{Murdock2017} introduced a measurement framework for directional novelty
in time-ordered text corpora: for each document, compare its token distribution
to aggregated distributions of past documents (prospective surprise) and
future documents (retrospective surprise). Their signed difference --- positive
when a document is unusual against its recent past and close to its near
future --- they call \emph{resonance}. The framework was developed on Charles
Darwin's reading notebooks and extended in \citet{Barron2018} to French
Revolution parliamentary debates. Both corpora share a salient property: the
text is authored by the agent whose novelty is the object of measurement, and
the author's cognitive or rhetorical state is what the framework is designed
to track.

This paper applies the same apparatus to a qualitatively different corpus.
The goods/services descriptions of US trademark filings are
\emph{commercially-incentivised}, typically drafted by counsel rather than the
firm principal, and exist to define legal protection scope --- not to express
cognition or rhetoric. The text is subject to two competing pressures: it must
be wide enough to cover the applicant's intended commercial activity and
narrow enough to be granted by an examiner. Whether the resonance framework
produces interpretable structure on text of this kind is the empirical
question of this paper.

The empirical record in text-based economics has converged on text-similarity
methods --- the text-based network industry classification of
\citet{HobergPhillips2016} being the most influential --- and has rarely used
trademark text for measurement. Trademark filings have been used to study
business formation \citep{Dinlersoz2018} and to distinguish protective
filings from substantive product introductions \citep{Graham2013}, but the
text of those filings has been largely unused.

The paper's claims are bounded by what the data support. The constructed
signal \dkl{}, computed as in \citet{Murdock2017, Barron2018}, produces three
empirical patterns on a 2.32 million-filing granted corpus 1990--2024 across
four NICE classes. Cross-industry vocabulary diffusion is directly traceable
at the theme level, with directional asymmetry: software- and tech-services-
origin themes arrive in transport and advertising/retail years later, not the
reverse. The diffusion structure is method-invariant under two independently
fitted topic models, though the methodological space within which I test
invariance is restricted to bag-of-words matrix factorization. Within firms,
\dkl{} is orthogonal-to-negative with patent counts, establishing the
construct as empirically distinct from patent-track invention. At the
filing-and-token level, \dkl{} predicts post-registration §8 maintenance in a
U-shape that is robust to operationalization but whose mechanism is not
separable with the trademark record alone.

What the paper does not claim. \dkl{} does not measure innovation in any
strong sense. The within-firm patent-orthogonality refutes the
``\dkl{} replaces patents as an innovation measure'' reading; the framework's
status as commercially-incentivised text refutes the cognitive-state reading
inherited from Darwin's notebooks; and the small-$n$ validation against
expert ``most innovative'' lists ($n=48$ matched firms) is too weak to
license the strong claim. What \dkl{} measures, on this evidence, is
\emph{lexical resonance} on commercial text: a filing's forward-leaning
position in its industry's vocabulary trajectory, with interpretable
empirical consequences that are documented and bounded below.

A worked methodological discipline is folded into Section~\ref{sec:rq4}: the
Schumpeter Mark~I read of new themes (entrant-led innovation) collapses on
year-matched baseline contrast. The methodological point is that the
trademark-text literature is particularly exposed to base-rate bias because
the corpus is mechanically saturated in entrants, and the discipline of
year-matched contrast is what distinguishes useful from inflated findings.

Section~\ref{sec:construct} describes the construct, the corpus, and known
fragilities. Section~\ref{sec:transit} presents the cross-industry phrase
transit table. Section~\ref{sec:ushape} reports the U-shape in registration
outcomes at the filing and token levels. Section~\ref{sec:kill} reports the
kill-condition tests and their methodological scope. Section~\ref{sec:patent}
reports the within-firm patent orthogonality. Section~\ref{sec:rq4} reports
the Schumpeter Mark~I baseline collapse. Section~\ref{sec:scope} states
scope. Section~\ref{sec:repro} is reproducibility.

\section{\dkl{} on a USPTO trademark corpus}\label{sec:construct}

\subsection{The DeDeo apparatus}

For a time-ordered sequence of documents, with each document at year $t$
producing a token distribution $P_t$, the DeDeo framework defines two
quantities against a window $W$ of past and future documents
\citep{Murdock2017, Barron2018}:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textit{Prospective surprise}: $\mathrm{KL}(P_t \,\|\, Q_{\mathrm{past}})$, where $Q_{\mathrm{past}}$ is the aggregate token distribution of documents in years $t-W$ to $t-1$.
  \item \textit{Retrospective surprise}: $\mathrm{KL}(P_t \,\|\, Q_{\mathrm{future}})$, where $Q_{\mathrm{future}}$ is the aggregate distribution of documents in years $t+1$ to $t+W$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The signed resonance is
\[
\Delta\mathrm{KL}_t \;=\; \mathrm{KL}(P_t \,\|\, Q_{\mathrm{past}}) - \mathrm{KL}(P_t \,\|\, Q_{\mathrm{future}}).
\]

Positive \dkl{} marks a document whose vocabulary is unusual relative to
recent past and close to near future. Negative \dkl{} marks the inverse,
language that looked normal at filing and looked played-out in hindsight.

\subsection{Corpus}

The USPTO Trademark Annual Applications backfile (product TRTYRAP) provides
a complete record of US trademark filings from 1884 onward. For this paper I
work with four NICE classes selected to exercise canonical cross-industry
diffusion: software (009), scientific and technical services (042), transport
(039), and advertising and retail (035). The selection follows the framing
that vocabulary born in software/tech and arriving in physical-economy
classes is a canonical phenomenon worth measuring directly.

Filing counts in each class, restricted to filings with registration\_date
populated (the grant-conditioning rule below), from 1990 onward: software
912{,}408; tech-services 575{,}041; transport 100{,}885; advertising/retail
753{,}050. Pooled, 2{,}317{,}899 granted filings 1990--2024 enter the
diffusion analyses.

\subsection{Grant conditioning}

The corpus is restricted to filings with a populated registration date. The
conditioning has two motivations. First, applications include speculative or
defensive filings that may not reflect realized commercial activity; the
registration outcome screens out applications that did not survive
examination. Second, the resonance signal is most interpretable for filings
whose vocabulary reflects intended commercial use rather than maximal
claim-scope drafting. Approximately 56\% of Class~009 filings in the full
backfile are granted; the four-class pooled share is similar. Grant
conditioning also discards a substantial fraction of negative-\dkl{} filings
that may merely be unconcluded applications.

\subsection{Computation}

For each granted filing at year $t$ in class $c$ with goods/services text
producing token distribution $P$ (unigram + bigram, after a standard analyzer
that drops stopwords and tokenizes within punctuation chunks), the
prospective and retrospective reference distributions $Q_{\mathrm{past}}$ and
$Q_{\mathrm{future}}$ are computed from all granted filings in class $c$ in
the respective windows. I use $W = 5$ years (flat window) with Dirichlet
smoothing ($\alpha = 1$ per cell) to avoid infinite surprise from
zero-count tokens. The within-class vocabulary is the set of unigrams and
bigrams with document frequency $\geq 50$.

\subsection{What the construct does and does not measure}

The construct's known fragilities have been documented in companion analysis
on a complete Class~009 corpus of 1{,}807{,}636 filings. Prospective and
retrospective KL are correlated at $r = +0.971$ across 1.56M scored filings;
signed \dkl{} is therefore a small residual of two large, highly correlated
quantities. \emph{This residual is noisy at the per-filing level.} The
magnitude $|\Delta\mathrm{KL}|$ inflates monotonically for short filings
(0.293 for documents under 5 terms, decaying to 0.147 for documents of 40 or
more terms) --- a length artifact that requires control in any per-filing
analysis. Pre-1995 readings are contaminated by thin past (mean \dkl{} of
$+2.05$ in 1990); the burn-in cut should exclude these years.

Window sensitivity is bounded. Across $W \in \{3, 5, 7\}$ years, only
6.9--10.4\% of filings flip sign --- the direction of the residual is robust
to half-life kernel choice, the magnitude is kernel-relative. This is well
under conventional fragility thresholds of 20--30\% sign-flip.

What the construct measures, on this evidence, is \emph{lexical resonance}: a
filing's forward-leaning position in its industry's vocabulary trajectory at
filing time. The construct does not measure innovation in the strong sense.
Sections~\ref{sec:transit}--\ref{sec:patent} examine what the construct
produces empirically and what those patterns license.

\section{Cross-industry phrase transit}\label{sec:transit}

\subsection{Operationalization}

To make cross-industry diffusion operational without committing to a specific
topic-extraction method (Section~\ref{sec:kill} takes up the topic-extraction
question), I define a list of 14 multi-token business-model phrases and track
their prevalence across (class, year) cells directly. Phrase membership is
established by case-insensitive regex match on the raw goods/services text,
which preserves multi-word phrases that the analyzer's stopword removal would
mangle. For each phrase, I record the first year in which each class crosses
a 1\% prevalence floor as the ``arrival'' year for that theme in that class.

Of the phrases that clear the 1\% floor in transport and advertising/retail,

exception is on-demand, which originates in transport (2014) before
software/tech (2016--17). The asymmetry --- software/tech vocabularies
arriving later in physical-economy classes more often than the reverse ---
is concrete and replicable from the published code.

\subsection{Caveats}

The 1\% floor is sensitive to class size. Transport (039) has roughly
one-eighth the filing volume of software (009), so the floor trips on far
fewer absolute filings in transport. A phrase below 1\% in transport may
still be present with substantive absolute volume. The 1\% choice is
conventional; a different floor (0.5\% or 2\%) shifts arrival years by a
year or two but does not reverse the directional pattern.

The asymmetry is also a four-class result. A full 45-class build with a
NICE-to-NAICS crosswalk would test whether software/tech-as-net-exporter is
a class-size artifact or a substantive directional pattern. The current
evidence is consistent with the directional reading but cannot exclude a
corpus-construction alternative.

\section{The U-shape in post-registration outcomes}\label{sec:ushape}

\subsection{Filing-level result}

Among granted filings in the post-burn-in 1996--2020 window with $\geq 10$
terms, 5-year post-registration §8 maintenance is U-shaped in \dkl{}. Filings
whose \dkl{} sits in the extreme positive tail and filings whose \dkl{} sits
in the extreme negative tail both maintain registration 5--10 percentage
points more often than filings whose \dkl{} sits near zero. The result holds
under multiple outcome operationalizations: examiner approval, reach-and-still-
live at 5 years, and (in a smaller subsample) a SEC-listed-firm proxy. The
filing-level lift is moderate but consistent across operationalizations.

\subsection{Token-level result}

The U-shape is sharper at the token level. \emph{Marks containing tokens at
the extreme tails of within-class vocabulary trajectory survive substantially
better than marks containing flux-neutral middle tokens.} Examples:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
\item Tokens at the negative tail --- vocabulary that was disappearing from
the class as the field moved on (\emph{typesetting}, \emph{dial-up},
\emph{e-liquid}, \emph{pre-craft beer}) --- appear in marks that survive at
50--80\%.
\item Tokens at the positive tail --- vocabulary that was emerging
(\emph{cannabis}, \emph{crypto}, \emph{vape}, \emph{NFT}, several
AI-era tokens) --- appear in marks that survive at 50--82\%.
\item Tokens in the flux-neutral middle (\emph{organisational},
\emph{conformity}, \emph{freighting}, \emph{various}) appear in marks that
survive at 11--26\%.
\end{itemize}

The token-level gap is 30--50 percentage points. The filing-level statistic
and the token-level statistic are different measurements: the filing-level
statistic bins filings by their \dkl{} score; the token-level statistic
identifies tokens at the resonance tails of within-class vocabulary
trajectories and reports survival of the marks that contain them. Both
patterns are evidence of the same underlying phenomenon, but the magnitudes
should not be conflated.

\subsection{Robustness and mechanism}

The U-shape is robust to substitution of the outcome operationalization
(examiner approval, §8 maintenance at 5 years, currently-live at 10 years),
to choice of half-life kernel within $W \in \{3, 5, 7\}$ years, to length
control, and to class. The pattern is present in 32 of the 44 NICE classes
that have sufficient post-1995 volume to estimate.

Mechanism is the harder question. Three candidate stories cannot be
separated with the trademark record alone:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
\item \emph{Intelligent harvesting} at the negative tail: applicants
deliberately register sunsetting categories when competition has departed,
maintain the marks for residual revenue, and survive because the marks face
little contest.
\item \emph{Passive late-occupation}: applicants register category-typical
language inherited from before the field moved on, are not contested for the
same reason, and survive by inertia rather than design.
\item \emph{Selection on something unobservable}: the kinds of applicants
who file at the tails differ on capitalisation, sophistication, or other
covariates that independently predict mark maintenance.
\end{itemize}

The trademark record does not separately identify which of these is the
dominant mechanism. The empirical pattern --- squeeze on the centre, lift at
both extremes --- is robust; the causal interpretation is not. Token-level
operationalizations of ``survival'' (the 50--80\% versus 11--26\% gap above)
suggest the pattern is not driven by a small number of high-volume registrants
at the tails, but the cross-firm identification of mechanism is owed to
follow-up work.

\section{Kill conditions on the diffusion structure}\label{sec:kill}

The phrase transit in Section~\ref{sec:transit} depends on a curated phrase
list. A different list, or a fully data-driven topic extraction, could produce
a different picture. This section tests whether the diffusion structure is a
property of the corpus or an artifact of the phrase-list choice or the
topic-extraction method.

\subsection{LDA topic extraction}

I fit a Latent Dirichlet Allocation topic model with $T = 50$ topics on a
stratified sample of 200{,}000 granted filings (50{,}000 per class, capped at
class size; transport is smaller after sampling). The vocabulary is unigrams +
bigrams with document frequency $\geq 200$, giving $V = 69{,}361$ terms. The
fitted model is transformed across the full 2.32 million-filing pool.

The resulting top-word lists are recognizable business-model components.
Selected examples: T0 \{health, care, wellness, healthcare\}; T1 \{autonomous,
loading, unloading, truck, material handling\}; T2 \{store, retail,
wholesale\}; T10 \{electronic transmission, computer networks, data,
communication\}; T30 \{online, website, non-downloadable, information\}; T49
\{vehicles, traffic, bicycle, lights\}. I make no claim that 50 unsupervised
topics constitute the final theme set; cross-method stability and human
face-validation remain owed before any individual theme is treated as
headline.

\subsection{Shuffle null}

For each (theme, class) prevalence trajectory over 1990--2024 --- where
prevalence is the share of granted filings in that class-year whose top topic
is that theme --- I compute the lag-1 temporal autocorrelation. Real themes
should ramp up smoothly within classes (positive autocorrelation); random
permutations of (class, year) labels across filings should average near
zero. I permute four times.

The real panel's themes ramp up smoothly within classes; the shuffled panel
is noise around zero. The null --- that filings are randomly distributed over
(class, year) cells --- is rejected at any plausible significance level.

\subsection{Scope of the shuffle test}

The shuffle test rejects label randomness but does not by itself establish
that the panel structure \emph{is} diffusion of business-model vocabulary.
A weaker reading is consistent with the data: the panel has whatever
non-random structure follows from applicants choosing classes and dates being
filing dates. A stronger reading --- that themes diffuse across industries
with substantive directional content --- requires the additional structure
that themes which appear earlier in one class predict appearance in another,
which the transit table of Section~\ref{sec:transit} provides, and which the
flow asymmetry of Section~\ref{sec:flow} below quantifies. I report the
shuffle test as confirmation that the diffusion-shaped structure I observe is
not an artifact of arbitrary panel slicing, not as proof that the structure
is diffusion in the absence of the substantive evidence elsewhere.

\subsection{NMF independent replication}

LDA is a generative topic model with specific distributional assumptions. To
test whether the panel structure depends on these assumptions, I refit topic
structure with non-negative matrix factorization on the same 200{,}000-filing
sample and vocabulary, with $T = 50$, then transform across the full pool.

The two models partition the corpus differently in detail. The adjusted Rand
index between LDA top-topic and NMF top-topic on a 500{,}000-filing sample
is $+0.214$: modest but well above random partition. Greedy Jaccard matching
on top-10 words gives 28\% of NMF topics aligning with an LDA topic at
Jaccard $\geq 0.3$ and 14\% at $\geq 0.5$. Seven of ten hand-curated seed
phrases from Section~\ref{sec:transit} (blockchain, cloud, AI/ML, SaaS,
mobile-app, AR/VR, marketplace) have a clear best-NMF-topic; the three
missing (streaming, IoT, on-demand) are either too generic or too brief at
the phrase level to concentrate in a single topic at $T = 50$.

\subsection{Method invariance, and the test that remains owed}

LDA and NMF are both bag-of-words matrix factorizations on the same
vocabulary. They differ in objective and convergence path but share the
structural assumption that documents are bags of tokens. The agreement
between them establishes that the diffusion-shaped panel structure is not
specific to either matrix factorization, but it does not establish that the
structure is robust across methodologically distinct topic-extraction
paradigms.

The cross-paradigm test --- sentence-embedding clustering (e.g., a public
model such as \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) followed by clustering, or
graph-based community detection over filing-token bipartite structure --- is
the natural methodological extension. It is not run in this paper. If the
cross-paradigm test produces a substantially different panel structure on
the same corpus, the method-invariance claim of this paper would need to be
qualified.

\subsection{Bass-fittable adoption curves}

For each (theme, class) cell where a theme reaches the 1\% prevalence floor
and is tracked for at least six years post-arrival, I fit the Bass (1969)
diffusion model to the normalized cumulative prevalence. The Bass model
parameterizes adoption with an innovation coefficient $p$, an imitation
coefficient $q$, and a saturation level $m$ \citep{Bass1969}. I fit $p$ and
$q$ with non-linear least squares, retaining fits with $R^2 \geq 0.7$.

Of 118 retained fits, the median $p$ is 0.017, the median $q$ is 0.114, and
the median $q/p$ is approximately 7. The median $q/p$ falls in the range
typically reported for consumer-product Bass fits, lending external
plausibility to the (theme, class) adoption curves as describing real
adoption events rather than topic-model artifacts. The bulk of fits (88 of
118) are platform-like (broad gradual adoption, moderate word-of-mouth); a
small tail of 9 fits shows fad-shaped curves (high $q/p$).

\subsection{What the kill conditions earn}

The kill conditions establish that the cross-class diffusion structure
is real in the corpus and not an artifact of phrase choice, label
assignment, or specific topic extraction within the bag-of-words paradigm.
They do not establish that any individual LDA or NMF topic is a substantively
meaningful business-model theme; the cross-method stability filter
excludes 72--86\% of LDA topics from candidate-headline status. They also
do not establish corpus-property invariance across cross-paradigm topic
extraction methods. The diffusion structure stands; the methodological
extension owed is the cross-paradigm test.

\section{Empirical distinctness from patents}\label{sec:patent}

If \dkl{} and patent counts were noisy measures of the same underlying
construct, they should correlate within firms over time. They do not.

The within-firm analysis uses a published panel of 174{,}569 firm-years
covering 14{,}463 trademark owners that have been name-matched to PatentsView
patent assignees \citep{PatentsView}. The panel is constructed in companion
work and used here only to validate the construct against the patent-track
measure. I fit a two-way (firm + year) fixed-effects regression of
standardized firm-year mean \dkl{} on $\log(1 + \mathrm{patents})$ with
firm-clustered standard errors. The standard deviation of mean \dkl{} on the
collapsed firm-year panel is 0.214.

\quad\quad\quad\ \ patents$_{t}$ & $-0.019$ & $0.010$ & $-1.9$ \\
\quad\quad\quad\ \ patents$_{t+1}$ & $+0.010$ & $0.010$ & $+1.0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The coefficient is negative and precisely estimated, strengthening to
$-0.051$ under filing-count control. The strongest negative coefficient sits
on patents$_{t-1}$: in years a firm patents more, the firm's subsequent
trademark vocabulary regresses toward class-typical rather than ahead.

The within-firm result establishes that \dkl{} is empirically distinct from
patent-track invention. It does not by itself license a strong claim that
\dkl{} measures a different innovation channel. An alternative reading
consistent with the within-firm data is that firm activity allocation
between patent prosecution and brand work shifts year-to-year and the
trademark vocabulary becomes more boilerplate during years of high patent
output. The empirical distinctness is robust; the substantive interpretation
of the distinct channel is bounded by the validation evidence elsewhere.

\section{A worked discipline result: Schumpeter Mark~I collapses on baseline contrast}\label{sec:rq4}

This section reports a methodological result more than a substantive one,
because the trademark-text literature is particularly exposed to base-rate
bias on questions where raw shares are interpretable as evidence.

The classical Schumpeter Mark~I~/~II debate asks whether new innovations are
predominantly born from new entrants (Mark~I) or from incumbent
diversification (Mark~II) \citep{Schumpeter1942}. The standard empirical
form is to identify a population of early adopters of an innovation,
classify them as entrant or incumbent, and report the entrant share.

I apply this standard form to the 50 LDA themes. For each theme, I take its
50 earliest carriers (sorted by filing year, conditional on top-topic weight
$\geq 0.20$). I classify each carrier's owner as an entrant if its first
granted mark across the four-class corpus is in the carrier-filing year or
one year earlier --- that is, the carrier is filing at or near the owner's
debut.

\begin{table}[h]\centering\small
\caption{Raw entrant share among the 50 earliest carriers per theme.}
\label{tab:raw_schumpeter}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Statistic & Value \\
\midrule
Themes scored & 50 \\
Mean entrant share among 50 earliest carriers & 73.7\% \\
Themes entrant-dominated ($\geq 50\%$ entrants) & 48 / 50 \\
Themes incumbent-dominated ($\leq 20\%$ entrants) & 0 / 50 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent The raw numbers look like strong Schumpeter Mark~I. This is the
result that does not survive baseline contrast.

The corpus's overall debut rate among granted filings in 1990--2024 is
68--77\% per year across the four classes. With a year-weighted mean
(weighting by each theme's earliest-carrier year distribution), the baseline
is 76.4\%. \emph{Most filings in the four-class corpus over this period are
by owners debuting that year.}

\begin{table}[h]\centering\small
\caption{Schumpeter~I with year-matched population baseline.}
\label{tab:rq4_baseline}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Statistic & Value \\
\midrule
Theme entrant share (mean / median) & 73.7\% / 73.0\% \\
Year-matched baseline (mean / median) & 76.4\% / 76.7\% \\
\textbf{Excess (theme $-$ baseline)} & $\mathbf{-2.7}$pp / $-3.7$pp \\
Themes with positive excess & 22 / 50 \\
Themes with excess $> 10$pp & 9 / 50 \\
Themes with negative excess & 28 / 50 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The mean theme excess is $-2.7$ percentage points; 28 of 50 themes are
\emph{below} baseline. Only nine themes show real entrant excess above
10pp. The Schumpeter Mark~I claim collapses: themes inherit the corpus's
high debut rate without adding to it.

The methodological point: the trademark-text literature is particularly
exposed to base-rate bias because the corpus is mechanically saturated in
entrants, and any analysis that reports raw population shares without
year-matched baseline contrast risks reading mechanical corpus structure as
substantive finding. I report this result not because Schumpeter Mark~I is
the central question of the paper but because the discipline of base-rate
contrast is what distinguishes useful from inflated findings in this kind of
analysis, and the discipline should be visible in any work that uses
trademark text to ask population-share questions.

\section{Scope and limits}\label{sec:scope}

The paper makes the following claims.

\dkl{}, computed as in \citet{Murdock2017, Barron2018} with a 5-year flat
window on granted USPTO trademark filings, produces a directly traceable
cross-industry vocabulary diffusion record on a 4-class subset of the corpus.
The directional asymmetry --- software/tech-origin themes arriving in
transport and advertising/retail with multi-year lags, the reverse rarely
happening --- is concrete at the phrase level and replicates at the theme
level under two independently fitted topic models on the same vocabulary.

\dkl{} predicts post-registration §8 mark maintenance in a U-shape at both
the filing and token levels, with the token-level gap larger than the
filing-level gap and the two should not be conflated. The U-shape is
robust to operationalization; the underlying mechanism is not separable with
the trademark record alone.

\dkl{} is empirically orthogonal-to-negative with patent counts within firm,
confirming the construct is distinct from patent-track invention.

The Schumpeter Mark~I read of new themes does not survive year-matched
baseline contrast, and the discipline of base-rate contrast is what makes
the difference.

The paper does not make these claims.

\dkl{} does not measure innovation in any strong sense. The defended
primitive is lexical resonance on commercial text. The strong-sense ``\dkl{}
measures innovation'' reading is contradicted by the within-firm patent
orthogonality and is not licensed by the small-sample validation evidence
($n = 48$ matched firms on BCG/MIT lists, reported in companion construct-
validity work but not in this paper).

\dkl{} does not identify a return premium on extreme-tail debut filings;
an earlier reported $+28$pp per $\sigma$ four-year debut excess return is a
small-sample right-tail artifact and is dropped.

The shuffle null does not prove that the panel structure is diffusion; it
rejects label randomness. The substantive content of the diffusion reading
is supplied by the transit table, the flow asymmetry, and the Bass-fittable
curves.

The LDA/NMF method invariance does not establish corpus-property invariance
across cross-paradigm topic extraction. The cross-paradigm test (sentence-
embedding cluster or graph community detection) is the natural methodological
extension and is not run here.

The four-class subset cannot exclude class-size or corpus-construction
explanations for the software/tech-as-net-exporter asymmetry; a 45-class
build with NICE-to-NAICS crosswalk is the natural extension.

What would falsify the diffusion claims. The cross-class transit asymmetry
reversing under a 45-class build with proper class-size controls. The
shuffle null failing to be rejected under a structurally different topic-
extraction method. The seed-phrase rediscovery under NMF dropping well
below 4 of 10 at $T = 50$ with this vocabulary.

What would falsify the patent orthogonality. The within-firm coefficient
reversing under value-weighting or an alternative trademark-owner-to-patent-
assignee match.

What would falsify the U-shape. The shape disappearing under joint
length-and-volume control on a class-by-class basis, or appearing only in
classes where the maintenance-base-rate is also U-shaped in firm
characteristics not controlled here.

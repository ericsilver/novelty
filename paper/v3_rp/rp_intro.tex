\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The literature that reads trademarks as innovation data counts them:
filings, classes, breadth, renewals. The only part of a filing it uses to
place a firm in an industry is the Nice class code. The goods/services
description itself --- a forced, dated disclosure of what the firm
expected to sell, written also by the millions of firms that never patent
--- has gone essentially unread against time, and where text has entered
the literature it has entered piecemeal: as breadth counts, as similarity
scores, as vocabularies hand-curated for single studies. There is more in
the text than the class. This paper reads it systematically, and measures
two things the class code cannot carry: how unusual a description is for
its industry, the filing's \emph{atypicality}, and whether its wording ran
ahead of or behind where the industry's wording was moving, its
\emph{lead}.

The construction and its validation are the paper's first contribution.
All 13.99 million USPTO case files since 1884 are re-parsed into
event-dated prosecution histories; each description is reduced to themes
by a topic model fitted once over the corpus, and its theme mix is
compared, by Kullback--Leibler divergence, to its own class in the five
years before and after its filing date. The validation program varies
everything a critic would vary --- three partitions of the vocabulary,
two reference weightings, five window constructions, the text-duplication
that pervades a boilerplate-heavy corpus --- on identical filings. Lead
survives all of it. Atypicality does not: its apparent effects can belong
to the partition (a global model reads a theme imported from another
class as atypical) or to a control, and the paper documents two hazards
that any trademark-text measure inherits --- word-level scoring measures
description length and drafting style, and pooling granted with abandoned
filings mixes the examiner's selection with the market's.

A measure is worth the outcomes it can be tested against, and the record
supplies dated, high-stakes ones: registration, the sworn five-year proof
of continued use whose failure means the product left commerce, the
year-ten renewal, and the owner's appearance in SEC filings. These let
the validated measure answer an old question --- whether pioneers are the
ones with arrows in their backs --- on a record where being early is read
directly from what every entrant wrote, not from the survivor-prone
samples of named markets \citep{GolderTellis1993, SemadeniAnderson2010}.
The answer is that the arrows are real. Among 3.10 million registrations,
marks whose language most led their industry were cancelled at the
five-year proof $1.4$ points more often than marks whose language most
lagged it, within Nice class and registration year with drafting and
counsel held --- $2.3$ points raw, in 33 of 44 classes
(Section~\ref{sec:gate}). The penalty is episodic where language moved
fastest: two to three times larger for marks filed in the late 1990s,
reversed for 2000--2004 filings, and returned from 2008, with the swing
inside four technology classes (Section~\ref{sec:waves}). Following the
owner instead of the mark, neither axis of the debut's language predicts
a subsequent priced private round; SEC reporting and listing decline in
lead; and the highest-revenue firms the record can trace to SEC filings
debuted in language their industries already used
(Section~\ref{sec:success}).

The paper proceeds from measurement to application.
Section~\ref{sec:related} places the exercise and settles its
nomenclature against the terms the innovation literature already uses.
Section~\ref{sec:measure} builds the measure and states the record's
gates and conventions. Section~\ref{sec:registration} treats
registration --- where atypicality is rewarded up to a point and lead,
computed from filings not yet made, could not be observed by anyone.
Sections~\ref{sec:gate} and~\ref{sec:waves} carry the five-year proof and
its movement across industries and eras; Section~\ref{sec:success} the
firm outcomes. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} reads the stages together and
states the implications for trademark-based measurement;
Section~\ref{sec:robust} summarizes the validation program, with the full
treatment in the supplementary material. The event-dated corpus, every
artifact, and interactive per-class exploration tools are public.

Being early with the language of a product costs at the five-year proof
of continued use, in three of every four product classes and under every
partition of the vocabulary, and it costs most in the industries and the
years in which the language was moving fastest. Being unusual is rewarded
at the same proof, mostly where a theme is imported from elsewhere, and
it is the examiner, not the market, that rewards middling conformity at
registration. The firms that thrived were not the pioneers of language.
They were written in the words their industries already used, and they
are few.

# novelty
Innovation is fundamental to human progress, yet disruptive innovation shrinks some markets while creating others and is thus poorly measured via standard economic metrics.  As a consequence, this driving force of human prosperity remains elusive for research and challenging for policy-makers to encourage. Current efforts to operationalize innovation are divided between qualitative measures, quantitative measures, and infrequent attempts to integrate them with proprietary data.
Qualitative measurements of innovation include interview-driven measures such as Gatignon’s (2002) innovation scale based on interviews with R&D Directors, Arundel’s Community Innovation Survey (CIS)(Smith; 2005), and guidance from the OECD in the Oslo manual (1997).  Survey data highlights the broad challenge of categorizing innovation, recognizing that “When we talk about [firm innovation], we are not only speaking of financial investments, but on the use of human capital to think, learn and solve complex problems and to produce qualitatively different types of innovations” (Arundel; 1997).   Compounding this issue, it has proven difficult to identify units of analysis which has made the accretion and combination of studies of innovation difficult.  In 2002, Gatignon et al. wrote: “Innovation and technical change are at the core of dynamic organizational capabilities, yet after more than 30 years of research on innovation and organizational outcomes, fundamental concepts and units of analysis are often confused and/or ambiguous.  As a result, empirical results are often inconsistent or difficult to reconcile.”
In the remainder of that widely-cited paper, Gatignon and his co-authors offered a scale that sought to codify and rate a level of innovation at the level of the firm.  This scale contains numerous trade-offs that were dictated by available technology: survey data demonstrated common variance bias, restricted sample size, and data sharing restrictions prevented the dissemination and improvement of the resulting model.  Further, the usefulness of self-reported data is limited in that any wide use of the scale for comparison may lead to motivated misrepresentation.  
	In contrast, quantitative measures have broadly categorized companies according to their “innovativeness” which they have operationalized via patent application counts (Schmookler; 1962, Zvi; 1990), citations per patent (Brownwyn, Jaffe, Trajtenberg; 2005), unlikely patent citation combinations (Uzzi; 2013), stock market value in excess of earnings (Kormendi, Lipe; 1987), and net R&D spend.  These measures introduce other problems: how patents are used is not stable over time, across industries, or within firms.  Trajtenberg (1990) criticized the use of patents broadly and his suggested fix of focusing on patent citations seems to have fallen out of favor since it was found that the majority of patent citations are discovered post-hoc in the patenting process (Juan, Gittelman; 2006).  Koellinger (2008) demonstrated that measures of innovation and profits were not correlated in e-business in Europe suggesting that approaches that use stock market prices to operationalize innovation are flawed.  Indices which are based on net R&D spend capture only explicit spending on invention, not the processes or cultures throughout the company.
	Efforts to integrate the insights of quantitative and qualitative measures of innovation have been limited.  Hagedoorn and Cloodt (2003) proposed a composite construct for ‘innovative performance’ based solely on quantitative factors.  They claimed that their construct also captured qualitative factors because their factor analysis demonstrated significant correlation among R&D inputs, patent counts, patent citations and new product announcements, but no demonstrated correlation with other measures of innovation.  I suggest that these inputs only weakly operationalize company innovativeness and see their correlation as saying that each is as weak as any of them.  A more direct correlation is based on R&D spend generating patents and patents generating citations and products, independent of their novelty.  Bruno, Daguet and Mairessec (1998) related firm survey data via the European Community Innovation Survey comprised of innovative product sales to firm innovation output operationalized via patent count and innovative sales.  Despite some of their data coming from company surveys, however, it remained quantitative data and did not bridge quantitative and qualitative approaches. 
	Machine learning makes it possible to consider qualitative data sources as quantitative inputs.  Here I seek to identify a mechanism by which qualitative data contained in trademark filing, patent, and financial filings can generate a novelty score.  Over time, other companies replicating these novel behaviors will be used as a signal that they were not merely novel but also also innovate.  Traditional approaches utilizing patents and equity returns will also be considered and integrated insofar as they are helpful.  

Method of the first study:
	The basic mechanism of our first study is to use a dated corpus of trademark filings from numerous firms to identify the components of company business models and to analyze the novelty of those components to arrive at a company-specific score indicating business model novelty.  NGram analysis is typically used to identify the occurrence of key words or phrases within a corpus of documents.  We propose a modification where Wordnet clustering analysis is first used to identify likely phrases occurring in each corpus, per Hotho, Staab, and Stumme (2003).  By discarding the most common phrases, the resulting set of phrasal clusters can be filtered to reflect only the technologies that are used to describe these entities without editorial input and without the manual creation of an index.  This limits editorial bias and will enable the algorithm to be re-used as additional trademark data becomes available over time.  These “phrasal clusters” will describe markets, customer segments, technologies and technology domains, and distribution channels.  
	Trademark data is particularly desirable because it is brief, descriptive of businesses, and application authors are incentivized to accurately describe the scope and functions of their businesses.  There is an incentive in writing a Trademark filing to cover all intended uses of the Trademark with some specificity since being more generic than the business weakens the trademark.  Appendix 1 presents selected trademark filings and identifies statistically improbable phrases which may be inter-related between these filings.  Also evident are the non-highlighted phrases which will likely be contained in numerous filings and so will be excluded from analysis.  Our analysis will weight each phrase should be weighted by its prevalence within the description so that companies with longer trademark filings are not inherently judged as more novel than companies with shorter descriptions.
	The US Patent and Trademark office publishes updates to trademarks nightly and via a partnership with ReedTech they host historical data since 1984 in XML format.  There are roughly 400,000 new applications filed each year, suggesting that our analysis may be run against 12 million records.  Prior to 1984 they have scanned images of trademarks, but document quality limits the ability to utilize optical character recognition without generating errors.  These errors would generate unique character strings that would be judged as highly novel by my algorithm and would invalidate the results.  Not all companies file for trademarks, but since most innovative companies imagine the potential dissemination of their innovations, we imagine that innovative companies are adequately represented in the trademark data.
“Surprise” is a concept in information theory that measures how unexpected a phrase is.  Surprise can be measured via a Bayesian model progressing in chronological sequence through the total corpus of company documents.  Within each document, the presence of each other phrase indicates the “surprise” inherent in each other phrase.  This is equivalent to the “distance” metrics used by Uzzi to identify unusual citation combinations in patents, but applied directly to the phrases that describe business models.  These phrases then update the Bayesian model such that each phrase and each combination of phrases becomes less surprising when it is next encountered which also renders all other combinations marginally more surprising.    
Another name for “surprise” is Shannon entropy, a log-normed measure of how much information is implicit in a finding.  Because of the mathematics of this approach, the first time something is encountered is it considered infinitely surprising.  Since we wish to create a comparable score, we will consider every event to be the N+1th example to eliminate infinite surprise and to make log transformed surprise additive.  In order to generate this complete list of potential phrasal clusters, we must first go through the entire dataset solely to identify possible phrases and to seed our consideration algorithm with a single example of each.  This log-normed entropy measure will be scaled by the prevalence of the phrase in the company-specific corpus and the remaining slices will be added together to composite the novelty of the description of the business.
Since we have no data before 1984, we will not be able to evaluate companies until some years after that period.  The delay is necessary since the oldest trademarks will be judged without developed priors and will seem extremely novel, despite likely being common to their period.  Despite this restriction, we will have over ten million records that we are able to analyze to determine their novelty.  
The goal of this first study is to create a “novelty score.”  This score is not competitive with existing work, but instead is a direct measure of entropic “surprise”.  Because many of these methods are unfamiliar to many in the social sciences, it also seems prudent to recruit participants through MTurk to evaluate recent trademark filings and to indicate their own measure of novelty.  This novelty measure should correlate with the algorithmic results obtained from the Bayesian model and should be briefly presented in the first paper.

Method of additional studies:
	Though we being by describing business model novelty, our goal is to establish how innovative company business models are.  Translating this “novelty measure” into a measure of innovation requires that it be scored; qualitative approaches (Gatignon) have identified success as the difference between novelty and innovation.  In this conception, I will measure the “success” of a technology or process as its replication in the marketplace.  Thus, a company with high novelty of business concept would be ruled “innovative” if the elements of their business concept were replicated after their formation.
	The ambition of this research program is not solely to add another metric by which to measure the novelty of firms, but instead to extend this operationalization of novelty to an innovation score that transcends prior work.  The second paper will seek to translate the novelty score to an innovation score by scoring individual business sub-concepts with equity market returns.  In order to establish construct validity and to demonstrate that this isn’t merely a confused presentation of self-evident equity returns posturing as “innovation”, comparisons to existing work are planned to address content validity, criterion validity, and construct validity respectively:
1.	Establishing content validity will be challenging as previous researchers have generally published their regression coefficients, but not their data nor their complete results.  As an example, see Calantone, Cavusgil and Zhao (2002, 2003), or Saloman and Shaver (2005). In these three cases, however, the authors provided their methodology and regression coefficients for “learning orientation”, “firm innovation capability” and “degree of innovativeness” respectively.  By replicating their methods with a small subset of firms and comparing their results we should be able to measure the content validity of our scale as a measure of “innovativeness.”  While it would be desirable to compare only to a single dataset, there is no broad agreement around measures of innovation and for content validity to be widely accepted we must cover sufficient ground of conceptual agreement to capture the majority opinion.
2.	Joaquin Alegre’s work with Lapiedra and Chiva (2006) with the French biotech industry focused on product innovation performance and utilizes only publicly data and can thus be fully replicated and updated.  As a consequence of the 2013 French sunshine act, French biotech product data is fully disclosed, creating a data corpus which can be scored against the proposed algorithm.  From this result, regression will provide a function to translate results from Alegre’s construct and our operationalization of innovation allowing us to assess our criterion validity by translating directly from his scores to our own and measuring their correlation.  
3.	I will seek to establish construct validity by evaluating hypotheses against the construct including a prediction that company novelty increases risk as measured by the likelihood that novel trademarks are less likely to be renewed, that innovative companies that survive are more likely to see above-market returns as measured by post-IPO returns, that high-innovation companies will be more likely to cluster geographically near other high-innovation companies, and that there will be a significant but not perfect correlation between our innovation measure, patent counts, patent citations, and other traditional measures of innovation.
Since I anticipate that future work will refine this measure and wish for it to be widely available, I plan to make not only the output of this algorithm but also the algorithm itself available online.  The code will be written in Python which is freely available, hosted on Github without cost as an open source project, and accessible via an open API.  Updates to the algorithm should be introduced to establish criterion validity and maintain construct validity.  Calls to the API should be able to specify a version number so historical experiments can be replicated exactly. 
	A criticism of the novelty approach may be that it is sensitive to the phrases that someone uses to describe their business.  As a consequence, the earliest innovators may not have exposure to yet-to-be coined words and phrases that would describe their innovation.  One potential bridge is to extend this analysis to additional data sources: job postings, the resumes of founders, KickStarter listings, AngelList angel company profiles, 10-k and other filings to regulators, investor communication, and company web pages.  Each of these sources will have their own vagaries: patent language may indicate the hand of a patent lawyer, trademarks are not always filed or updated, LinkedIn profiles are inconsistently filled out and updated, and 10-k filings limit our analysis to the public statements of public companies.  And yet, there is no reason to think that this noise would be correlated, so a composite measure should outperform each individual measure.  This removes some noise from our measure of novelty.  Combining these scores should also be more indicative of company novelty (and by extension company innovativeness) than business model novelty.

Expected results:
	Machine learning is often a trade-off between human readability of the data and its predictive power.  Novelty scores, however, are intended to be usable by other researchers and thus must be a single number.  Shannon “surprise” is communicated in terms of bits of information where more surprising findings are represented by more bits. These, then, are scaled by the prevalence of the surprising phrase within the company-specific text.  The first outcome, novelty, may be a very small number which is difficult to process, so we ought to report it as the inverse of the log.  This results in a number greater than zero that communicates how novel the description of the business is.  This number translates readily into a “percent similar” statistic which indicates what percentage of businesses had a similar business model and can be scaled against industries, projected forward or backwards in time or directly compared with the scores of other companies.
	Extending this novelty number to a measure of “innovativeness” requires the number be scaled to combine the insights of other researchers.  The mechanism to produce this number may be a non-linear transformation, blending of multiple sources, separate algorithm to address the error, or a combination of all of these.  While this approach is exploratory, I hypothesize that a blended consideration of multiple data-sets will yield findings such as individual technologies or market orientations that are highly correlated with future profitability, that the quickest and slowest to innovate will underperform the market, and that there will be an advantage to fast followers.  Further, I hypothesize that while this measure will correlate with existing measures, the existing measures are sufficiently flawed that the correlation will resemble the 0.3 coefficient common between existing measures.
	Established literature in the space suggests a linear monotonic relationship between innovation and long-term business success.  This implies that in a model where R&D spend is used to operationalize innovation, a company that invests all of their revenue on research and development will also maximize profits.  To establish discriminative validity, this measure of company innovation should be able to demonstrate diminished returns at both extremes of R&D spending and equity market expectations – a measure of innovation that does not recognize that companies may over-invest in innovation may sell consulting services but it does not describe the world.
	In order to evaluate the nomological validity of this model we should be able to evaluate hypotheses regarding the benefits and costs of innovation in different market structures.  In disrupted industries, we should see that companies that have been scored as being more innovative should outperform their competition.  We can evaluate this by identifying disrupted industries and creating a binary disruption variable for purposes of ANOVA analysis.  We should see that the combination of “disruption” and “innovation” should correlate more strongly with business success than “disruption” and “not innovative”.  This is a blunt measure, but descriptive.  I further hypothesize that innovation would not be as beneficial in highly regulated industries and might similarly analyze highly regulated industries in isolation.  In both cases, our analysis would be aided by identifying industries that did not foresee their disruption or regulation when the representative cohort was being founded.
	By identifying the underlying components of business models it is my hope that researchers will be able to improve the estimation of business innovativeness.  One potential example of an improvement would be an analysis which recognizes that while some technologies are not novel in some industries, using those technologies within another industry carries a greater degree of novelty.  By first identifying these underlying components, we can track the adoption of new methods across industries.  This work also allows researchers to track new approaches to business within geographic clusters, across industries, measured against C-suite education and background, and make recommendations to policy-makers regarding the appropriate support of technology creation and dissipation support.  

Conclusion:
	Beyond failing to integrate qualitative and quantitative approaches, current innovation constructs also make it impossible to answer questions regarding the construct and the underlying measures: do patents aid innovation?  Are outstanding stock market returns driven by innovation or hampered by it?  Is there a degree of innovation which is harmful?  Is there an underlying cadence of optimal innovation which can be learned from?
	I came to this research question from the insight that there are underlying components to companies, that technology hype cycles lead to “me too” “research” which re-discovers, and from a genuine desire to separate companies that advance the human condition (and which should be supported by government) from monopolists whose sole legal mechanism to preserve legal monopoly is found in a series of incremental patents.  By operationalizing innovation as a scaled by-product of novelty, researchers will be able to ask better questions about technology adoption – the benefits, the enablers, the costs and the consequences.
	I forsee that there may be a negative reaction from those who have based their construal of “innovation” on patents or equity price premium.  There is a significant business in people selling innovation to businesses at significant premiums which I believe may be based on risky postulates.  If it is possible to demonstrate an “innovation sweet spot” which captures the point at which R&D spent is near-optimal per industry, this forms a marked contrast with those who have defined “innovation” using easily available metrics and then coupled those measures with pro-innovation philosophy.  It is a drawback of this proposed research program that our primary source of validity is in correlating our measure with existing measures.  I believe, though, that if data on business model component usage and dissipation were widely available, researchers studying companies would include it in their analysis.  Over time, this inclusion would help to identify the worst oversights of this research program and may lead to meaningful refinements.
